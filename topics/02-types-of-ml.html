Types of Machine Learning

Machine learning problems come in four families, and the family is decided by one question: what kind of feedback does the learner get?

Beginner≈ 45 min6 interactive demos

1. The four families at a glance

Four ways to learn.

  • Supervised: a student with a teacher and an answer key. Every practice question has a marked answer.
  • Unsupervised: an explorer with no map or teacher, sorting shells on a beach into piles that look alike.
  • Semi-supervised: a student given just a few solved examples and a huge pile of unsolved ones to figure out.
  • Reinforcement: training a puppy. Nobody shows the right move; you give a treat (reward) or a "no" (penalty) and it works out what to do.

2. Supervised learning

In supervised learning the model is trained on labelled data: every example comes with the correct answer. The model learns the mapping from inputs to outputs, then predicts the answer for new inputs it has never seen. "Supervised" means the answers act as a supervisor that says how wrong each guess was.

Supervised learning is split into two sub-types, depending on what the label looks like.

2a. Regression: predict a number

Regression predicts a continuous numeric value: something that can be any number in a range, with meaningful distances between values.

  • House price (rent) → ₹5,000, ₹7,500, ₹12,000
  • Temperature tomorrow → 25 °C, 30 °C, 18 °C
  • Salary of an employee → 30,000, 50,000, 80,000

Real-world uses: pricing houses from area, rooms and location; forecasting stock or commodity prices; weather forecasting; forecasting product sales for retail or e-commerce; estimating delivery time; predicting energy demand for a power grid.

Algorithms you will learn: Linear, Ridge and LASSO Regression; KNN, Decision Tree, SVM (SVR), Random Forest and Gradient Boosting also have regression versions.

2b. Classification: predict a category

Classification predicts a discrete class from a fixed list of options.

  • Binary: two classes: spam / not spam, fraud / genuine, disease / healthy.
  • Multi-class: many classes but exactly one is right: digit 0–9, flower species, type of loan.
  • Multi-label: several can be true at once: a movie tagged "action" and "comedy".

Real-world uses: spam filters, fraud detection, medical diagnosis, loan approval, sentiment analysis of reviews, face recognition, predicting customer churn.

Algorithms you will learn: Logistic Regression, Naïve Bayes, KNN, Decision Tree, SVM, Random Forest, AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost.

A common confusion: "Logistic Regression" is a classification algorithm. The name is historical. It outputs a probability, which is then cut into classes. Always decide regression vs. classification by the type of the label, not by the algorithm's name.

3. Unsupervised learning

Unsupervised learning works on unlabelled data: there is no "right answer" column. The model must find hidden patterns, structure or relationships on its own, without prior guidance.

The two big problem types are clustering and dimensionality reduction; a third, association rule learning, finds "things that go together".

3a. Clustering: group similar things

Clustering groups data points that look alike, using only their features. Data flows: input features → model (a scientific algorithm) → find similar patterns → interpret → Cluster 1, Cluster 2, Cluster 3….

  • Customers → Group 1, Group 2, Group 3 (customer segmentation for targeted marketing).
  • Animals → Cluster 1: cats & dogs · Cluster 2: lions & tigers.
  • Movies → Action · Romance · Comedy (recommendation).
  • News articles → Entertainment · Business · Sports · Politics.

Notice that you had to pick how many groups (K) and that the algorithm gave the groups only numbers (0, 1, 2), not names. Interpreting what a cluster means ("budget shoppers") is the human's job. The K-Means, DBSCAN and Hierarchical lessons cover this in depth.

3b. Dimensionality reduction: keep the information, drop the extra columns

The number of input features is the dataset's dimension. Dimensionality reduction reduces the number of features (variables) while keeping the important information. This simplifies the model, cuts computation and often improves performance.

Example: a house table has "number of bedrooms" and "total area of bedrooms", which carry nearly the same information. Combine them into a single feature.

BedroomsArea per bedroom (sq ft)PriceTotal area of bedroomsPrice

Bedrooms × area-per-bedroom gives the total bedroom area, so three columns became two (dimension 3 → 2) without losing what matters. Another example: a student's scores in 10 subjects can be reduced to a single total marks feature.

Why bother? Fewer columns mean faster training, less complexity and less over-fitting, and you can visualise data by squashing it down to 2D. In short: keep the important information, remove unnecessary features, lose almost no data.

"Information" here means spread (statisticians call it variance): how far apart the points are. Squash the points onto a line, then measure how spread out the shadows are:
share kept = spread of the shadows ÷ total spread of the original points.
The direction that keeps the biggest share is the first principal component. Finding it is called PCA (Principal Component Analysis), which gets a full lesson later. LDA is a supervised cousin that keeps the directions that separate classes best.

3c. Association rule learning: things that go together

Finds rules such as "customers who buy bread and butter often also buy jam" by counting how often items appear together in transactions (market-basket analysis). It powers "frequently bought together" boxes, store shelf layout and cross-selling.

Real-world unsupervised learning: banks group customers by behaviour to design products; Spotify clusters listeners with similar taste; retailers analyse baskets; security teams flag unusual network traffic (anomaly detection); data teams use PCA to compress hundreds of sensor readings before modelling; biologists cluster genes with similar activity.

4. Semi-supervised learning

Labels are expensive: a doctor must annotate each scan, a linguist each sentence. Yet raw, unlabelled data is cheap and plentiful. Semi-supervised learning trains on a small labelled set plus a large unlabelled set. The labelled examples say what the classes are; the unlabelled examples reveal the shape of the data (which points cluster together), which pins down where the boundaries should go.

A teacher solves just two problems on the board (one of each kind) and hands you 100 unsolved ones. You notice the unsolved problems fall into two visibly similar batches, so you copy the solution style to each batch. Two labels plus lots of structure are enough.

The key assumption: points that are close to each other, or connected through chains of neighbours, probably share a label. Semi-supervised methods (Label Propagation, Label Spreading, self-training) use exactly this. They get their own lessons later.

Real-world uses: medical imaging (few expert-annotated scans, thousands of unlabelled ones); speech recognition; web-page classification; labelling text or images at scale; fraud detection where only a few cases are confirmed.

5. Reinforcement learning (RL)

In reinforcement learning there is no dataset. An agent learns by interacting with an environment: it takes an action, sees what happens, and receives feedback as a reward or penalty. Its goal is to learn a strategy that maximises the total reward over time.

  • A reward is given when the action is good or desired: it moves the agent closer to the goal, follows the correct rule or behaviour, or produces a positive outcome.
  • A penalty is given when the action is bad or undesired: it moves the agent away from the goal, breaks a rule or causes failure, or produces a negative outcome.

The trick that makes RL work is Q-learning: keep a table Q(state, action) = "how good is this action here in the long run", and update it after every step.

In words: new score = old score + α × ( what I just learned − what I believed ), where what I just learned = reward + γ × (the best score available from the next spot).

Worked example. α = 0.5, γ = 0.9. The robot in state s moves right, gets reward r = −0.2 and lands in state s′ whose best Q-value is 6. Old Q(s, right) = 2.
Target = r + γ·max Q(s′) = −0.2 + 0.9 × 6 = 5.2.
Error = 5.2 − 2 = 3.2.
New Q = 2 + 0.5 × 3.2 = 3.6. Repeat this thousands of times and good values ripple back from the goal to every cell.

Explore vs. exploit. ε (epsilon) is the chance the agent tries a random action instead of the best-known one. With ε = 0 it never discovers better routes; with ε = 1 it never uses what it learned. Good RL balances both.

Real-world uses: game playing (chess, Go, video games; AlphaGo); robotics (path planning, grasping, movement control); self-driving cars (lane changes, braking); data-centre cooling and energy control; recommendation ordering and ad bidding; and fine-tuning large language models with human feedback (RLHF).

6. All four side by side, in one product

Real products mix the families. A music streaming app might use:

Supervised

Predict whether you will skip a song (label: skipped yes/no) or predict the number of minutes you will listen tomorrow.

Unsupervised

Cluster millions of listeners by taste to create "listeners like you"; use PCA to compress audio features.

Semi-supervised

Only a few thousand songs have a human-assigned mood; propagate the mood tags to the millions of untagged ones.

Reinforcement

Choose the next song in an auto-playlist; reward = you listened to the end, penalty = you skipped.

A fifth term you will hear: self-supervised learning. The labels are generated from the data itself (hide a word in a sentence and predict it). Large language models are pre-trained this way. It sits between unsupervised and supervised and is beyond the scope of this classical-ML course, but now you know where it fits.

Key takeaways

  • Supervised = labelled data. Numeric label → regression; category label → classification.
  • Unsupervised = no labels. Find groups (clustering), fewer columns (dimensionality reduction), or co-occurring items (association rules).
  • Semi-supervised = few labels + many unlabelled points, and neighbours share labels.
  • Reinforcement = an agent learns by trial, reward and penalty, and the output is a policy.
  • Pick the family by asking: "What feedback do I have?"
