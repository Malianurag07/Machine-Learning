Gradient Boosting

Start with a crude guess. Look at the mistakes you made. Train a small tree to predict those mistakes, add a small fraction of it to your guess, and repeat. Each tree nibbles away at what is left over, and hundreds of small corrections add up to one of the most accurate models for table-shaped data.

1. The big idea: fix the leftovers

AdaBoost improved its ensemble by re-weighting the points that were misclassified. Gradient Boosting takes a more general route: at every round it asks "what error is still left?" and trains the next small tree to predict exactly that error. The prediction of the whole model is simply the starting guess plus a small step from every tree.

Golf. The first shot goes roughly toward the hole. Now the ball lies 60 metres short. The second shot is not aimed at the hole from the tee; it is aimed at the remaining 60 metres. It lands 15 short. The third shot covers those 15. Each shot only fixes what the previous ones left over. And a sensible golfer does not swing at full power every time: taking measured, small shots (the learning rate) gives more control near the hole than a few huge swings that overshoot.

The recipe (regression)

  1. Start with a constant prediction: the average of y.
  2. Compute the residual of every training point: residual = actual − current prediction.
  3. Train a small tree (depth 1 to 5) to predict the residuals from the features.
  4. Update: new prediction = old prediction + learning rate × tree's output.
  5. Repeat steps 2 to 4 for the chosen number of trees.

The final model is: F(x) = start + learning rate × (tree₁(x) + tree₂(x) + … + treeM(x)).

Why is it called "gradient"? Suppose the score for a point is the squared error ½ × (actual − prediction)². How should the prediction change to lower the score fastest? Its negative gradient (the downhill direction) is exactly actual − prediction, the residual. So "fit a tree to the residuals" is the same as "take a step downhill on the loss, in the direction the trees can express". For other loss functions the residual is replaced by that loss's own negative gradient, and the recipe is unchanged. That one idea lets gradient boosting handle regression, classification, ranking and more.

2. The maths by hand: regression

Six points, and we use stumps (depth-1 trees) with learning rate 0.5. Every tree splits the x-axis in two and predicts the average residual on each side.

Round 0: the start. The average of y is (2 + 4 + 3 + 9 + 11 + 10) ÷ 6 = 6.5. Every prediction is 6.5. Mean squared error (MSE) = 77.5 ÷ 6 = 12.92.

Round 1. Residuals = y − 6.5: −4.5, −2.5, −3.5, +2.5, +4.5, +3.5. The best single split of these residuals is x ≤ 3.5 (total squared error 4.0; the other four splits give 53.2, 40.8, 29.5 and 62.8). Left average = (−4.5 − 2.5 − 3.5) ÷ 3 = −3.5, right average = +3.5. Update with learning rate 0.5: left predictions 6.5 + 0.5 × (−3.5) = 4.75, right 6.5 + 0.5 × 3.5 = 8.25. MSE falls to 3.73.

Round 2. New residuals = y − prediction: −2.75, −0.75, −1.75, +0.75, +2.75, +1.75. Best split again x ≤ 3.5: left average −1.75, right +1.75. Update: 4.75 + 0.5 × (−1.75) = 3.875; 8.25 + 0.5 × 1.75 = 9.125. MSE = 1.43.

Round 3. Residuals: −1.875, +0.125, −0.875, −0.125, +1.875, +0.875. Now a different split wins: x ≤ 4.5 (squared error 2.92). Left (points 1 to 4) average = −0.6875, right (points 5, 6) = +1.375. Update: points 1 to 3 → 3.875 − 0.344 = 3.531; point 4 → 9.125 − 0.344 = 8.781; points 5, 6 → 9.125 + 0.6875 = 9.8125. MSE = 0.72.

The error drops by half or more every round; a smaller learning rate would make it fall more slowly. A new point with x = 5 would be predicted 9.81; x = 2 → 3.53.

Why not learning rate 1? With a rate of 1, round 1 would already jump to 3 and 10, the averages of each side. That looks great on six points but is exactly the problem: every tree would trust its own fit completely, including the noise. Taking only a fraction of each tree's suggestion ("shrinkage") forces the model to make progress through many small, cautious steps, and works better on new data. The price: you need more trees.

  • Round 0 is a flat line; the residuals are the data with the average removed.
  • Step the rounds up slowly with depth 1 and watch the purple next-tree line (right) become smaller and smaller as the residuals shrink.
  • Set the learning rate to 1 and depth to 4, and run past 40 rounds: the pink curve starts threading through individual noisy points.

3. Learning rate versus number of trees

The two knobs work as a pair: a small learning rate needs many trees. Below, three ensembles are trained on the same data, differing only in learning rate. Each curve shows the error on 120 unseen points as trees are added.

What to look for. The orange curve (rate 1) plunges fastest, hits its lowest point within a handful of trees, then rises steeply: it has begun memorising noise. The green curve (rate 0.05) needs many more trees (usually tens to over a hundred) to reach its lowest point, but that point is as good or better, and it climbs back up much more slowly afterwards. The purple curve (0.3) sits in between. Press "New random data" a few times: the pattern repeats, though the exact numbers change. In practice: choose a small rate (0.05 to 0.1) and let a validation set (or early stopping, see below) decide how many trees to use. Unlike a random forest, adding trees to a boosted model can overfit.

4. Gradient boosting for classification

For a yes/no target the trees still predict numbers. The model keeps a running score in log-odds (the same scale logistic regression uses; see that lesson), and converts it to a probability with p = 1 ÷ (1 + e−score). The "error" that trees chase is again "actual minus current prediction", now residual = y − p where y is 0 or 1 and p is the current predicted probability.

Same six x-values, labels 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1 (three of each class).

Start. The share of 1s is 3 ÷ 6 = 0.5, so start score = ln(0.5 ÷ 0.5) = 0 and every point has p = 0.5. The score for the model (log-loss = −[y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p)], averaged) is ln 2 = 0.693.

Residuals = y − p: −0.5, −0.5, +0.5, +0.5, −0.5, +0.5.

Round 1. Fit a stump to those residuals. Best split: x ≤ 2.5 (squared error 0.75; the alternatives are 1.2, 1.33, 1.5, 1.2). Now the leaf values. A leaf could just be the average residual, but for log-loss the standard leaf value is a slightly cleverer "Newton step":

leaf value = (sum of the residuals in the leaf) ÷ (sum of p × (1 − p) in the leaf)

In words: the total correction the leaf's points want, scaled by how uncertain they still are (p × (1 − p) is largest when p is near 0.5). Left leaf (points 1, 2): (−0.5 − 0.5) ÷ (0.25 + 0.25) = −1 ÷ 0.5 = −2. Right leaf (points 3 to 6): (0.5 + 0.5 − 0.5 + 0.5) ÷ (4 × 0.25) = 1 ÷ 1 = +1.

With learning rate 1, the new scores are −2 (points 1, 2) and +1 (points 3 to 6). Converting: p = 1 ÷ (1 + e2) = 0.119 for points 1 and 2; p = 1 ÷ (1 + e−1) = 0.731 for points 3 to 6. Log-loss = (2 × 0.127 + 3 × 0.313 + 1.313) ÷ 6 = 0.418, down from 0.693. (Point 5 is the mislabelled-looking one, a 0 surrounded by 1s: it costs 1.313 and gets a large residual of −0.73 for the next tree to work on.)

Round 2 repeats the whole thing with the new residuals (y − new p). After more rounds the probabilities sharpen towards 0 and 1, but with a learning rate below 1 they do so in gentler steps.

5. Gradient Boosting in scikit-learn

Our regression example (the depth-1 trees and rate 0.5 are set to match the hand calculation):

import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor, GradientBoostingClassifier

X = np.arange(1, 7).reshape(-1, 1)
y = np.array([2, 4, 3, 9, 11, 10.])

gb = GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=3, max_depth=1, learning_rate=0.5, random_state=0).fit(X, y)
for step in gb.staged_predict(X):     # the prediction after 1, 2, 3 trees
    print(step.round(3))
# [4.75  4.75  4.75  8.25  8.25  8.25 ]
# [3.875 3.875 3.875 9.125 9.125 9.125]
# [3.531 3.531 3.531 8.781 9.812 9.812]   <- our hand calculation (9.8125 is shown as 9.812 by numpy's rounding)

# the classification example: labels 0 0 1 1 0 1, one stump, learning rate 1
yc = np.array([0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1])
clf = GradientBoostingClassifier(n_estimators=1, max_depth=1, learning_rate=1.0).fit(X, yc)
print(clf.decision_function(X))        # [-2. -2.  1.  1.  1.  1.]   the log-odds we computed
print(clf.predict_proba(X)[:, 1])      # [0.119 0.119 0.731 0.731 0.731 0.731]

Now a realistic run on the breast-cancer data. The comments are from an actual run with scikit-learn 1.9.0; other versions and seeds may differ a little.

from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, cross_val_score
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, stratify=y, random_state=42)

gb = GradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=42).fit(X_train, y_train)   # 100 trees, depth 3, rate 0.1
print(gb.score(X_test, y_test))                                          # 0.958
print(cross_val_score(GradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=42), X, y, cv=5).mean())   # 0.963

# 50 trees at rate 0.1 scored 0.944 on the test set; 200 trees scored 0.958

# early stopping: let it stop when a held-out slice stops improving
gb = GradientBoostingClassifier(n_estimators=1000, learning_rate=0.1,
                                validation_fraction=0.2, n_iter_no_change=10,
                                random_state=42).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(gb.n_estimators_, gb.score(X_test, y_test))    # 130 trees were enough; test 0.951

All the boosted variants reach the same ballpark as the random forest (0.958) on this easy dataset: with only 143 test rows, one row is worth 0.7 percentage points, so differences of one or two rows mean little. The big gains of boosting show up on harder, larger datasets. Regression on the noisy diabetes data (R² on the same split as the Random Forest lesson):

from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor

# default settings (100 trees, depth 3, rate 0.1)                        R² = 0.215
# lower rate, shallower trees, and row sub-sampling
gb = GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=100, learning_rate=0.05, max_depth=2,
                               subsample=0.8, random_state=0)           # R² = 0.315
# (the random forest from the previous lesson scored 0.250 on this split)

Here careful settings beat both the defaults and the forest. That is typical: gradient boosting has more knobs than a forest, rewards tuning, and punishes careless defaults.

Parameter / attributeWhat it does
n_estimatorsNumber of trees (default 100). Too many + a big rate = overfitting.
learning_rateShrinks each tree's contribution (default 0.1). Lower is safer but needs more trees. Tune together with n_estimators.
max_depthDepth of each tree (default 3). Keep small: boosting wants weak learners. 2 to 5 is typical.
subsampleFraction of rows used per tree (default 1.0). Values like 0.5 to 0.8 ("stochastic gradient boosting") add randomness like a forest does and often help.
max_featuresFeatures considered per split, as in a random forest.
min_samples_leafMinimum rows per leaf: stops leaves that fit a handful of noisy points.
lossRegressor: "squared_error" (default), "absolute_error", "huber" (robust to outliers), "quantile". Classifier: "log_loss" (default).
validation_fraction, n_iter_no_changeEarly stopping: hold out part of the data and stop when it stops improving. Then n_estimators_ holds the number actually used.
staged_predict, staged_predict_probaPredictions after each tree: use them to plot a validation curve and pick the number of trees.
feature_importances_Impurity-based importance, as in a forest. (In one run on the breast-cancer data a single feature, "worst radius", took 56%.)

A sensible recipe: start with learning_rate=0.05 to 0.1, max_depth=3, subsample=0.8, a generous n_estimators plus early stopping, then tune depth and min_samples_leaf with cross-validation (Grid Search CV is a lesson coming up).

6. Strengths, limits and real-world use

StrengthsLimits
Often the most accurate method on tabular dataMany settings to tune; careless defaults can underperform a forest
Flexible: any differentiable loss (robust, quantile, ranking...)Trees are built one after another, so training cannot be parallelised across trees
No feature scaling; handles non-linear patterns and interactionsCan overfit noise if too many trees or too large a rate; sensitive to label noise and outliers (unless a robust loss is used)
Feature importances; works on mixed feature types once encodedHarder to interpret than one tree; cannot extrapolate beyond the training range

🔍 Web search ranking

Boosted trees trained with ranking losses (such as LambdaMART) have powered the ordering of search results.

🏦 Credit risk and fraud

Boosted trees on transaction and account features are a staple of banking and insurance models.

🚕 Demand and ETA forecasting

Predicting delivery times, prices or demand from tabular features: the "residual correction" style suits noisy numeric targets.

🏆 Data-science competitions

Gradient-boosted trees (in the XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost variants) have long been the go-to winners on tabular-data contests.

🛒 Click-through prediction

Will this user click this ad? Log-loss boosted trees give calibrated probabilities from many mixed features.

⚡ Insurance pricing

Regression with a quantile or Huber loss to predict costs while limiting the influence of a few extreme claims.

Key takeaways

  • Gradient boosting builds small trees one after another, each trained to predict the residuals (the negative gradient of the loss) of the model so far.
  • Prediction = start + learning rate × (sum of the trees' outputs). In the worked example the error fell 12.92 → 3.73 → 1.43 → 0.72 with three stumps.
  • For classification, the model keeps a log-odds score; the residual is y − p and leaves use (sum of residuals) ÷ (sum of p(1−p)).
  • A small learning rate with many trees generalises best; boosting can overfit as trees are added, so use validation or early stopping.
  • Keep trees shallow (weak learners), and consider subsample below 1. For big data use HistGradientBoosting*.
  • AdaBoost is a special case of the same idea (exponential loss, re-weighting points). XGBoost, next, is a faster, more regularised implementation.

Mini task (25 minutes, optional).

  1. Three houses cost y = 5, 7, 9 (in units of 10k). The starting prediction is the average. What is it, and what are the three residuals?
  2. A point has actual value 16 and current prediction 10. The next tree predicts 5.5 for it and the learning rate is 0.2. What is the new prediction and the new residual?
  3. Classification: the current score for a point is 0 and its true label is 1. What is p and what is the residual? After a leaf update makes the score +1, what are p (about 0.731) and the new residual?
  4. Two points sit in the same leaf, both with p = 0.5 and true label 1. Compute the leaf value using (sum of residuals) ÷ (sum of p(1 − p)), then the new probability with learning rate 1.
  5. Bonus: on the breast-cancer code, plot the test accuracy after each tree using staged_predict. Where does it peak, and how does that compare with the early-stopping result?
Show answers

1. Average = (5 + 7 + 9) ÷ 3 = 7. Residuals: 5 − 7 = −2, 7 − 7 = 0, 9 − 7 = +2.

2. New prediction = 10 + 0.2 × 5.5 = 11.1. New residual = 16 − 11.1 = 4.9. (Only a small step towards the target: the rest is left for later trees.)

3. p = 1 ÷ (1 + e0) = 0.5, residual = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5. After the update p = 0.731, residual = 1 − 0.731 = 0.269.

4. Residuals are 0.5 and 0.5, sum = 1. p(1 − p) = 0.25 each, sum = 0.5. Leaf value = 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2. New score = 0 + 2 = 2, so p = 1 ÷ (1 + e−2) = 0.881.

5. In my run, the best test accuracy (0.958) was reached at tree 74 of 100, and early stopping used 130 trees on a slightly different setup (0.951). On a test set this small, differences of one row are just noise; the point is that you do not need to use all the trees.
