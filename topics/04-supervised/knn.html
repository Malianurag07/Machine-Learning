K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)

KNN makes a prediction by asking a simple question: which K known examples look most like this new one? Then it lets them vote. There is no training phase and almost no math beyond measuring distance.

Beginner≈ 80 min4 interactive demos

1. The big idea

New in town. You move to a new neighbourhood and want to know if a restaurant is any good. You don't compute anything: you ask the K people who live closest to you and go with the majority. Or you want to guess the rent of a flat: look at the K most similar flats nearby and average their rents. "You are like your closest neighbours" is the whole algorithm.

  1. Store all the labelled examples. (That is the entire "training".)
  2. For a new point, measure its distance to every stored example.
  3. Pick the K closest ones (the neighbours).
  4. Classification: the neighbours vote and the majority class wins. Regression: predict the average of the neighbours' values.

KNN is called a lazy learner (or instance-based learner): it does no work when you "train" it, it just memorises the data, and does all the work at prediction time. It is also non-parametric: there are no weights θ to learn, and the decision boundary can take any shape.

2. Measuring "close": distance

To find neighbours we need a number for how far apart two points are. With features (x₁, x₂) for point A and (y₁, y₂) for point B:

Euclidean = the way a crow flies. Manhattan = the way a taxi drives through a grid of streets. Euclidean is the default; Manhattan can be more robust when there are many features or outliers. The steps are the same with more features: just keep adding terms.

Weighted voting: closer neighbours count more

In a plain vote every neighbour counts equally, even if one is far away. Distance weighting gives each neighbour a vote worth 1 ÷ distance, so nearer ones matter more. Take K = 3 from above:

Red: E at distance 1 → weight 1 ÷ 1 = 1.000.
Blue: C at 2 → 0.500, B at 3 → 0.333 → total 0.833.
Red (1.000) beats Blue (0.833), so the weighted answer is Red, even though the plain vote said Blue. Switch the "vote" button in the demo above to see it.

4. Choosing K: the bias–variance dial

  • Small K (1, 2, 3): follows every wiggle of the data, including noise and mislabelled points. The boundary is jagged: low bias, high variance, overfitting. With K = 1 the training accuracy is always 100% (each point is its own nearest neighbour), which tells you nothing.
  • Large K: averages over many points, so the boundary becomes smooth and stable, but it can wash out real detail: high bias, low variance, underfitting. With K = the whole dataset you always predict the most common class.

Rules of thumb: for two classes use an odd K so votes can't tie. A starting point is K ≈ √n (n = number of training points), but the reliable method is to try many values and pick the one with the best cross-validated score.

5. KNN is sensitive to scale: always standardise

Since KNN is all about distance, a feature measured in big numbers will dominate the distance, whether or not it matters. Salary in rupees (tens of thousands) will drown out age (tens). This is exactly the scaling problem from the Data Pre-processing lesson, and for KNN it is not optional.

In the demo, customers are described by age and income. In truth age decides who buys (young people buy), income is irrelevant. Ask the model about a 27-year-old earning ₹62k:

buysdoesn't buylines go to the K = 3 nearest neighbours

Standardising = (value − average) ÷ spread, computed per column, so age and income are both measured in "how many spreads from typical". Then a difference of "one spread" in age counts the same as one spread in income. (Details in the pre-processing lesson: StandardScaler.) Always fit the scaler on training data only.

6. KNN for regression

To predict a number, take the neighbours' values and average them (optionally weighted by 1 ÷ distance).

Example. Flats: 800 sq ft → ₹40 L, 1000 → 52, 1200 → 58, 1500 → 75, 1800 → 90. Predict a 1300 sq ft flat.
Distances: 1200 → 100, 1500 → 200, 1000 → 300, 1800 → 500, 800 → 500.
K = 2 → neighbours 1200 and 1500 → (58 + 75) ÷ 2 = ₹66.5 L.
K = 3 → add 1000 → (52 + 58 + 75) ÷ 3 = ₹61.7 L.

The prediction is a staircase. K = 1 passes through every point (overfits the noise); a large K gives a smooth, almost flat curve. Note that KNN regression can never predict values outside the range of the training targets, so it cannot extrapolate a trend.

7. Cost, speed and other practical issues

  • Prediction is slow, training is instant. A naive search compares the new point with all n stored points: cost ≈ n × (number of features) per prediction. For large data scikit-learn uses smarter structures (kd_tree, ball_tree) or you use approximate nearest-neighbour libraries.
  • Memory: the whole training set must be kept.
  • Curse of dimensionality. With many features (hundreds), all points end up almost equally far apart, so "nearest" stops meaning "similar". Reduce dimensions (PCA) or select features first.
  • Irrelevant features add noise to distances. Remove them.
  • Imbalanced classes: the majority class dominates the vote; use distance weighting or a smaller K.
  • Categorical features: encode them (one-hot) and scale, or use a suitable metric.
  • Missing values must be handled first (KNNImputer even uses KNN itself to fill gaps).

8. KNN in scikit-learn

Our hand example, reproduced:

from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

X = [[7, 8], [4, 1], [4, 2], [8, 6], [4, 3], [8, 8], [8, 5], [1, 6]]
y = ["Blue", "Blue", "Blue", "Blue", "Red", "Red", "Red", "Red"]

for k in (1, 3, 5):
    model = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k).fit(X, y)   # "fit" just stores the data
    print(k, model.predict([[4, 4]]))
# 1 ['Red']   3 ['Blue']   5 ['Red']

model = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=3).fit(X, y)
dist, idx = model.kneighbors([[4, 4]])      # who are the neighbours?
print(dist)                                  # [[1. 2. 3.]]
print(idx)                                   # [[4 2 1]]  -> points E, C, B
print(model.predict_proba([[4, 4]]))         # [[0.667 0.333]]  classes: Blue, Red

weighted = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=3, weights="distance").fit(X, y)
print(weighted.predict([[4, 4]]))            # ['Red']  (closer neighbours count more)

A realistic pipeline: scale, then search for the best K with cross-validation:

from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, GridSearchCV
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=42)

pipe = Pipeline([("scale", StandardScaler()), ("knn", KNeighborsClassifier())])
grid = GridSearchCV(pipe,
                    {"knn__n_neighbors": range(1, 31, 2),          # odd K only
                     "knn__weights": ["uniform", "distance"],
                     "knn__metric": ["euclidean", "manhattan"]},
                    cv=5)
grid.fit(X_train, y_train)

print(grid.best_params_)
print("test accuracy:", grid.score(X_test, y_test))
ParameterWhat it does
n_neighborsK. Default 5.
weights"uniform" (equal votes) or "distance" (1 ÷ distance).
metric, p"minkowski" with p=2 is Euclidean (default), p=1 is Manhattan; or metric="cosine" etc.
algorithm"auto", "brute", "kd_tree", "ball_tree": how neighbours are searched.
KNeighborsRegressorSame parameters; averages the neighbours' values.

🎬 Recommendations

"Users like you also enjoyed…": find the K most similar users (or items) and recommend what they liked.

✍️ Handwriting recognition

A classic benchmark: compare a digit image with labelled images of digits. Plain KNN gets roughly 97% on the MNIST digits.

🏥 Similar-patient search

Find past patients with similar tests and see how they responded to treatment.

🕵️ Anomaly detection

A point whose K nearest neighbours are all very far away is an outlier (fraud, faulty sensor).

🧩 Missing-value imputation

KNNImputer fills a gap with the average of the K most similar rows.

🔎 Semantic & vector search

Modern search, image lookup and AI "retrieval" systems are nearest-neighbour search on embeddings, KNN at enormous scale.

📍 Location services

"Nearest stores/drivers" is literally a nearest-neighbour query.

🏠 Property valuation

Estimate a home's price from the sale prices of the K most comparable homes ("comps").

Key takeaways

  • KNN stores the data, and predicts by voting (classification) or averaging (regression) among the K nearest points.
  • "Nearest" = smallest distance: Euclidean (√ of the sum of squared differences) by default, Manhattan (sum of absolute differences) as an alternative.
  • K is the key dial: small = flexible but noisy (overfit), large = smooth but blunt (underfit). Choose it by cross-validation; use odd K for two classes.
  • Always scale your features; remove irrelevant ones; beware many dimensions.
  • Distance weighting lets closer neighbours count more.
  • Training is instant, prediction is slow: fine for small/medium data, use tree-based search or approximate methods for big data.
