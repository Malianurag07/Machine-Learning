Naïve Bayes

Naïve Bayes classifies by playing detective: it weighs every clue, asks "how likely is this evidence if the email is spam, versus if it is not?", and picks the more probable answer. It is fast, tiny, and a classic for text.

Beginner≈ 75 min4 interactive demos

1. The big idea

Most classifiers learn a boundary. Naïve Bayes takes a different route: it learns how each class tends to look ("spam emails often contain the word free; ordinary emails often contain meeting"), and then, given a new email, asks which class would most likely have produced this? The tool for reversing "how does the class look" into "which class is this" is Bayes' theorem.

The detective. A detective starts with a hunch based on how common each kind of case is (the prior), then updates it with each clue found at the scene (evidence). A muddy footprint is common among gardeners and rare among bankers, so it shifts suspicion towards gardeners. Naïve Bayes does this with numbers, one clue at a time, and finally names the most likely suspect. It is called naïve because it assumes the clues are independent of each other, which is convenient but not always true.

2. Bayes' theorem in plain words

Write P(A | B) for "the probability of A, given that B is true". Bayes' theorem says:

$$P(\text{class}\mid\text{evidence})=\frac{P(\text{evidence}\mid\text{class})\times P(\text{class})}{P(\text{evidence})}$$
NameMeaningSpam example
Prior P(class)How common is the class before seeing anything?40% of all email is spam
Likelihood P(evidence | class)If the class were true, how likely is this evidence?Among spam, 60% contain "free"
Evidence P(evidence)How common is this evidence overall? (It just makes the answers add up to 100%.)How many emails of any kind contain "free"
Posterior P(class | evidence)What we want: the probability of the class after seeing the evidence.Given the email has "free", chance it is spam

In words: posterior = (likelihood × prior) ÷ evidence.

The surprising medical-test example

A disease affects 1% of people. A test catches it 90% of the time when it is present (sensitivity), and wrongly says "positive" for 9% of healthy people (false-positive rate). You test positive. How likely are you to be ill? Most people guess around 90%. The truth is far lower, and the easiest way to see it is to count real people:

Take 1,000 people.
• 1% are ill = 10 people. The test catches 90% → 9 true positives.
• 990 are healthy. 9% of them test positive → 89 false positives.
• So 9 + 89 = 98 people test positive, but only 9 of them are ill: 9 ÷ 98 = 9.2%.

Even after a positive test the chance is only about 9%, because the disease is so rare that false alarms outnumber real cases. That is the prior at work. (With Bayes' formula: 0.9 × 0.01 ÷ (0.9 × 0.01 + 0.09 × 0.99) = 0.0917, the same number.)

3. From one clue to many: the "naïve" step

An email has many words, and each is a clue. Ideally we would need P(all these words together | spam), but there are astronomically many word combinations, so we can never collect enough data for that. The naïve assumption saves us: pretend the clues are independent given the class. Then the joint likelihood is simply the product of the individual ones:

$$P(\text{spam}\mid w_1,w_2,\dots)\;\propto\;P(\text{spam})\times P(w_1\mid\text{spam})\times P(w_2\mid\text{spam})\times\cdots$$

In words: score for a class = its prior × (the chance each word appears in that class), all multiplied together. Do it for each class; the biggest score wins. To get proper probabilities, divide each score by the total of the scores.

The assumption is rarely true ("free" and "winner" often appear together), which is why it is called naïve, yet the ranking of classes is often still right, and it needs very little data.

Step 3: turn counts into probabilities, with a safety net. The raw estimate would be (count ÷ total words). But notice winner never appears in a ham email, and meeting never in spam. A probability of 0 would be a disaster, because multiplying by 0 wipes out every other clue (the zero-frequency problem). The fix is Laplace smoothing: pretend every word was seen once more than it really was:

P(word | class) = (count + 1) ÷ (total words in class + 4), where 4 is the vocabulary size.

WordP(word | spam)P(word | ham)
free(3+1) ÷ (8+4) = 0.333(1+1) ÷ (6+4) = 0.200
winner(3+1) ÷ 12 = 0.333(0+1) ÷ 10 = 0.100
money(2+1) ÷ 12 = 0.250(2+1) ÷ 10 = 0.300
meeting(0+1) ÷ 12 = 0.083(3+1) ÷ 10 = 0.400

Step 4: classify a new email: "free winner".

spam score = 0.5 × 0.333 × 0.333 = 0.0556
ham score = 0.5 × 0.200 × 0.100 = 0.0100
Total = 0.0656. So P(spam) = 0.0556 ÷ 0.0656 = 0.85 and P(ham) = 0.15. → Spam.

Now try "meeting money": spam = 0.5 × 0.083 × 0.25 = 0.0104; ham = 0.5 × 0.4 × 0.3 = 0.06; P(spam) = 0.0104 ÷ 0.0704 = 0.15 → ham.

Now do it yourself. Pick the words in a new email and watch each clue multiply in. Set the smoothing to 0 and include meeting to see the zero-frequency disaster.

A practical detail: use logs. A real email has hundreds of words, and multiplying hundreds of numbers below 1 gives a number so tiny that the computer rounds it to 0 (underflow). Real implementations add the logarithms of the probabilities instead; the ranking is identical. scikit-learn does this for you.

5. Why "naïve" can bite: repeated clues

Because the model multiplies clues as if each were new information, duplicated or highly correlated features are counted several times. If a clue that doubles the odds of spam appears as 5 copies (five features that all say the same thing), the model treats it as 2⁵ = 32 times stronger. The classification is often still right, but the probability becomes overconfident.

So treat Naïve Bayes probabilities as scores for ranking, not calibrated truths. (You can calibrate them afterwards with CalibratedClassifierCV.)

6. Continuous features: Gaussian Naïve Bayes

Words are counts, but many features are measurements (age, height, income). For those, Naïve Bayes summarises each class with a bell curve per feature: just the average and the spread (standard deviation) of that feature within the class. The "likelihood" of a new value is then the height of that class's bell curve at that value.

Example. Predict whether a customer will buy from their age, using equal priors. Buyers: average age 30, spread 5. Non-buyers: average 40, spread 6. A new customer is 34.
• Height of the buyer bell at 34 ≈ 0.0579 (34 is 0.8 spreads from 30).
• Height of the non-buyer bell at 34 ≈ 0.0403 (34 is 1 spread from 40).
P(buyer) = 0.0579 ÷ (0.0579 + 0.0403) = 0.59 → predict "buys", but only mildly confident.

With several features you multiply the bell-curve heights of each feature (the naïve step again).

7. Naïve Bayes in scikit-learn

Our spam example, exactly as computed by hand:

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB

emails = ["free winner", "free money", "free winner money", "winner",
          "meeting money", "meeting", "free meeting", "money"]
labels = [1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0]          # 1 = spam, 0 = ham

vec = CountVectorizer()                     # turns text into word-count columns
X = vec.fit_transform(emails)

model = MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0)            # alpha = the Laplace smoothing (the "+1")
model.fit(X, labels)

print(model.predict_proba(vec.transform(["free winner"])))     # about [[0.15 0.85]]  (ham, spam)
print(model.predict(vec.transform(["meeting money"])))         # [0]  ham

The probabilities match our hand calculation (0.85 spam). Two other everyday patterns: a text pipeline, and continuous data with GaussianNB.

8. Strengths, limits and when to use it

StrengthsLimits
Extremely fast to train (it just counts) and to predictIndependence assumption is usually false
Works with very little data and with many featuresProbabilities are over-confident and poorly calibrated
Handles many classes naturallyCan't learn interactions ("free" is fine, but "free" + "meeting" together is different)
Great baseline for text; easy to update with new dataUnseen feature values need smoothing; Gaussian version assumes bell-shaped data

Reach for it when: you need a quick, strong baseline; the data is text or high-dimensional and sparse; you need real-time predictions; or the training set is small. Prefer something else when feature interactions are crucial or you need well-calibrated probabilities (try logistic regression or gradient boosting).

9. Naïve Bayes in the real world

📧 Spam filtering

The original hit application: early filters like the ones popularised in the early 2000s were Naïve Bayes over words.

💬 Sentiment analysis

Is this review positive or negative? Word counts per class make an instant baseline.

📰 News & topic tagging

Sports, politics, business: each has its own tell-tale vocabulary.

🩺 Medical screening

Combine symptoms and test results as independent clues to rank likely conditions.

🌐 Language detection

Letter and word patterns differ by language; Naïve Bayes identifies the language of a snippet in microseconds.

⚡ Real-time & streaming

Because it only keeps counts, it can update on the fly, e.g. in a system learning from users flagging spam.

Key takeaways

  • Naïve Bayes applies Bayes' theorem: posterior = likelihood × prior ÷ evidence.
  • The naïve assumption (features independent given the class) lets it simply multiply the clues, so it needs very little data.
  • Score for each class = prior × product of P(feature | class); the biggest wins. Normalise to get probabilities.
  • Laplace smoothing (alpha) prevents a single zero count from wiping out all the evidence.
  • Choose the variant by feature type: MultinomialNB (counts), BernoulliNB (0/1), GaussianNB (continuous).
  • Great fast baseline, especially for text; treat its probabilities as rankings, not exact truths.
