Association Rule Learning

"Customers who buy bread and butter also tend to buy eggs." Association rule learning digs through thousands of shopping baskets (or any list of "things that happened together") and finds such if-then patterns automatically. There is no target to predict: it simply discovers what goes with what.

1. The big idea: what goes together?

The observant shopkeeper. After years behind the counter, a shopkeeper notices habits: people who buy pasta usually pick up sauce, and parents buying nappies often grab milk. He rearranges the shelves, offers discounts on pairs, and never runs out of the second item. Association rule learning is the shopkeeper's intuition done with arithmetic: count how often things occur together, and pick out the combinations that occur more often than chance would explain.

The data is a list of transactions (baskets). Each transaction is a set of items. The words to know:

TermMeaning
ItemsetAny group of items, e.g. {bread, butter}.
Rule X → Y"If a basket contains X, it also tends to contain Y." X is the antecedent (if-part), Y is the consequent (then-part). X and Y are itemsets with no item in common.

Like clustering and PCA it is unsupervised: there is no label; you decide what is "interesting" with thresholds. It is not limited to shops: the "items" can be symptoms in a patient record, web pages visited in a session, words used in a document, or products in a recommendation.

2. Three numbers: support, confidence, lift

A rule can only be judged with numbers. Suppose there are N baskets in total.

MeasureIn wordsFormula
SupportHow common is the itemset? The share of all baskets that contain it. Tells you if the pattern is common enough to matter.support(X → Y) = (baskets containing X and Y) ÷ N
ConfidenceHow reliable is the rule? Among the baskets that contain X, the share that also contain Y (a conditional probability).confidence = support(X and Y) ÷ support(X)
LiftIs the rule better than chance? How many times more likely Y is given X than Y is in general.lift = confidence ÷ support(Y)

How to read lift: lift = 1 means X and Y occur together exactly as often as if they were unrelated (independent). Lift > 1 means they occur together more often than chance: a real positive association. Lift < 1 means they occur together less often than chance: buying X makes Y less likely.

Counting: bread appears in 7 baskets, butter in 6, milk in 6, eggs in 5, cola in 2. Bread and butter appear together in baskets 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 and 10: 6 baskets.

Rule bread → butter. support = 6 ÷ 10 = 0.60. confidence = 6 ÷ 7 = 0.857 (of the 7 bread baskets, 6 also have butter). lift = 0.857 ÷ 0.60 = 1.43 (butter is in 60% of all baskets but in 86% of the bread baskets: 1.43 times more likely). A strong rule.

The reverse rule butter → bread. support 0.60 (the same, it is the same itemset), confidence = 6 ÷ 6 = 1.00 (every butter basket has bread), lift = 1.00 ÷ 0.70 = 1.43 (the same lift: lift is symmetric, confidence is not).

Rule milk → eggs. milk and eggs appear together in baskets 4, 5 and 8: support = 0.30. confidence = 3 ÷ 6 = 0.50. lift = 0.50 ÷ 0.50 = 1.00: eggs are in half of all baskets, and also in half of the milk baskets. Milk tells us nothing about eggs. A confidence of 50% looks decent, and lift shows it is only the base rate.

Rule eggs → milk. confidence = 3 ÷ 5 = 0.60, but milk is in 60% of all baskets, so lift = 0.60 ÷ 0.60 = 1.00. Again no real association.

Rule milk → bread. together in baskets 1, 5 and 9: support 0.30; confidence = 3 ÷ 6 = 0.50; lift = 0.50 ÷ 0.70 = 0.71: bread is in 70% of all baskets, but only in 50% of the milk baskets. Milk buyers are less likely than average to buy bread (in this tiny sample).

Why confidence alone is dangerous. If 90% of all baskets contain bags, then "anything → bags" has a confidence of about 90%, however unrelated the item is. Lift divides that base rate out. Two other measures you will see: leverage = support(X and Y) − support(X) × support(Y) (how many more baskets than expected under independence, as a share of N; zero means independent), and conviction = (1 − support(Y)) ÷ (1 − confidence) (how much more often the rule would be wrong if X and Y were independent; 1 means independent, larger is stronger, infinity means the rule never fails). For bread → butter: leverage = 0.60 − 0.70 × 0.60 = 0.18, conviction = 0.40 ÷ 0.143 = 2.8.

3. Finding frequent itemsets: the Apriori algorithm

The first task is to find every itemset that is common enough (support at least a chosen minimum support). The obvious way is to count every possible itemset, but a shop with n products has 2n − 1 of them: for 5 products 31, for 100 products about 1.3 × 1030, far too many to count.

The Apriori algorithm avoids most of that work with one simple fact, the Apriori property:

If an itemset is infrequent, every larger itemset that contains it is infrequent too. If {butter, milk} appears in only 2 baskets, then {bread, butter, milk} can appear in at most those 2 baskets (adding an item can never make a set more common). So once a small itemset fails the test, we can discard all its supersets without ever counting them.

So the algorithm works level by level:

  1. Level 1: count every single item. Keep the frequent ones.
  2. Level k: build candidate itemsets of size k by combining frequent (k − 1)-itemsets. Prune any candidate that has an infrequent subset (no need to count it). Count the surviving candidates in the data and keep the frequent ones.
  3. Stop when no candidates are left.

On our ten baskets with minimum support 0.3 (at least 3 baskets):

Level 1. bread 7, butter 6, milk 6, eggs 5, cola 2. Cola (2 < 3) is dropped: it can never be part of a frequent itemset.

Level 2. Candidates: the six pairs of the four frequent items. Counts: {bread, butter} 6 ✔, {bread, milk} 3 ✔, {bread, eggs} 3 ✔, {butter, milk} 2 ✘, {butter, eggs} 3 ✔, {milk, eggs} 3 ✔.

Level 3. There are four possible triples of the four items. Two of them, {bread, butter, milk} and {butter, milk, eggs}, contain the infrequent pair {butter, milk} and are discarded without counting (in fact {bread, butter, milk} occurs in only 2 baskets). The other two have all their pairs frequent, so they are counted: {bread, butter, eggs} occurs in baskets 3, 5 and 10: 3 ✔ frequent; {bread, milk, eggs} occurs only in basket 5: 1 ✘.

Level 4. The only candidate {bread, butter, milk, eggs} contains infrequent subsets: nothing to count. Stop.

Result: 10 frequent itemsets (4 single items, 5 pairs, 1 triple), found after counting only 5 + 6 + 2 = 13 itemsets instead of all 31.

Move the minimum support: with a lower threshold more itemsets survive and more levels are needed; with a high threshold everything is pruned early. Choosing this threshold is the main practical decision (see section 6).

4. From itemsets to rules

Once the frequent itemsets are known, rules are easy. Take each frequent itemset with at least 2 items and split it in every possible way into an antecedent X and consequent Y. For each split, compute the confidence (and lift), and keep the rules above a minimum confidence. (No new counting is needed: all the supports were already found.)

The six rules from {bread, butter, eggs} (support 0.30 in each case):

RuleConfidenceLiftReading
{butter, eggs} → bread0.30 ÷ 0.30 = 1.001.00 ÷ 0.70 = 1.43every basket with butter and eggs also has bread
{bread, eggs} → butter0.30 ÷ 0.30 = 1.001.00 ÷ 0.60 = 1.67strongest rule
{bread, butter} → eggs0.30 ÷ 0.60 = 0.500.50 ÷ 0.50 = 1.00no better than chance
butter → {bread, eggs}0.30 ÷ 0.60 = 0.500.50 ÷ 0.30 = 1.67butter makes the bread+eggs combination 1.67× more likely

Notice how three rules with confidence 0.5 or 0.6 have a lift of exactly 1.00: they look acceptable on confidence, but they carry no information. That is the point of lift.

Now scale up. This scatter plot shows every rule mined from the 400-basket shop as a dot: how common it is (support, horizontal) against how reliable (confidence, vertical); big pink dots have high lift. The sliders are the three thresholds. Try to isolate the shop's four hidden habits.

  • With the default filters a couple of dozen rules pass, and the top of the list is dominated by beer and chips combinations. Raise the minimum lift to 1.5: what survives is the real habits. Lower it to 0: hundreds of rules appear, most of them uninteresting.
  • To find negative associations (things that avoid each other), set minimum lift and minimum confidence to 0 and look for the pale-blue dots, the ones with lift below 1 (for example 🥤 cola with 🥛 milk).
  • Raise the minimum support to 0.2: only very common items survive, and the rare but strong rules (such as 🧷 diapers → 🥛 milk, whose itemset occurs in about 9% of baskets) disappear. This is the threshold trade-off in one slider.

5. Pitfalls

  • Too many rules. With many items and low thresholds you can get millions of rules. Some appear purely by chance (the more patterns you test, the more accidents you will find). Guard against this with a higher minimum support, a lift threshold, and by checking the rules on data from a different period.
  • Trivial and obvious rules. "Buys hot dogs → buys hot-dog buns" is true, and useless. The interesting rules are the ones you would not have guessed, and that someone can act on.
  • Association is not causation. Ice cream and sunscreen sell together, but ice cream does not cause sunscreen sales; the sun does. A rule shows that two things co-occur, not why.
  • Support threshold. Too high: you miss rare but valuable patterns (expensive items are bought seldom). Too low: an explosion of rules. Start high, then lower it.
  • Order and quantity are ignored. Only presence in the basket matters here. To take order into account (first this, later that), use sequential pattern mining.
  • The famous story. The tale of a store discovering that nappies and beer sell together is much repeated in textbooks, and may well be embellished; treat it as folklore and rely on your own data.

Faster algorithms: FP-Growth finds the very same frequent itemsets without generating candidates: it compresses the transactions into a tree (a frequent-pattern tree) and mines that. It is usually much faster on big data. (On our ten baskets I confirmed that Apriori and FP-Growth return identical itemsets.) Eclat is another variant.

6. Association rules in Python

scikit-learn does not include association rules. The standard tool is the mlxtend library (pip install mlxtend), which works on a table of True/False values, one row per basket and one column per item. The results below come from an actual run with mlxtend 0.25.0 and pandas (other versions may print slightly different columns).

import pandas as pd
from mlxtend.preprocessing import TransactionEncoder
from mlxtend.frequent_patterns import apriori, fpgrowth, association_rules

baskets = [["bread", "butter", "milk"], ["bread", "butter"], ["bread", "butter", "eggs"], ["milk", "eggs"],
           ["bread", "butter", "milk", "eggs"], ["milk"], ["bread", "butter"], ["milk", "eggs", "cola"],
           ["bread", "milk", "cola"], ["bread", "butter", "eggs"]]

te = TransactionEncoder()
df = pd.DataFrame(te.fit(baskets).transform(baskets), columns=te.columns_)   # one True/False column per item
print(df.sum())      # bread 7, butter 6, cola 2, eggs 5, milk 6     -> our counts

frequent = apriori(df, min_support=0.3, use_colnames=True)
print(frequent)
#    support  itemsets
#      0.7    (bread)
#      0.6    (butter)   0.6 (milk)   0.5 (eggs)
#      0.6    (butter, bread)
#      0.3    (eggs, bread)   (bread, milk)   (butter, eggs)   (eggs, milk)
#      0.3    (butter, eggs, bread)                      <- 10 frequent itemsets, exactly as we found by hand

rules = association_rules(frequent, metric="confidence", min_threshold=0.5)
print(rules[["antecedents", "consequents", "support", "confidence", "lift", "leverage", "conviction"]].round(3))
#  butter        -> bread, eggs    support 0.3  confidence 0.500  lift 1.667  leverage 0.12  conviction 1.40
#  bread, eggs   -> butter         support 0.3  confidence 1.000  lift 1.667  leverage 0.12  conviction inf
#  bread         -> butter         support 0.6  confidence 0.857  lift 1.429  leverage 0.18  conviction 2.80
#  butter        -> bread          support 0.6  confidence 1.000  lift 1.429  leverage 0.18  conviction inf
#  butter, eggs  -> bread          support 0.3  confidence 1.000  lift 1.429  leverage 0.09  conviction inf
#  milk          -> eggs           support 0.3  confidence 0.500  lift 1.000  leverage 0.00  conviction 1.00
#  ... (8 more, including eggs -> milk with lift 1.0 and milk -> bread with lift 0.714)      13 rules in total

strong = rules[(rules["lift"] > 1.2) & (rules["confidence"] >= 0.8)]      # keep only the interesting, reliable ones
same = fpgrowth(df, min_support=0.3, use_colnames=True)                   # FP-Growth: the same itemsets, faster on big data

All the numbers match the hand calculation: for example butter → bread has support 0.6, confidence 1.0 and lift 1.43, and the six rules from {bread, butter, eggs} include the two with lift 1.67. At min_support=0.2 there are 14 frequent itemsets instead of 10 (cola and {butter, milk}, {cola, milk}, {bread, butter, milk} join).

Function / argumentWhat it does
TransactionEncoderTurns a list of item lists into a True/False table (rows = baskets, columns = items).
apriori(df, min_support, use_colnames, max_len)Frequent itemsets with at least the given support. max_len limits the itemset size; use_colnames=True shows names instead of column numbers.
fpgrowth(...)Same result as Apriori, with a faster algorithm; the preferred choice for larger data.
association_rules(frequent, metric, min_threshold)Builds the rules and their measures: antecedent/consequent supports, support, confidence, lift, leverage, conviction. Filter by metric="confidence" or "lift" with a minimum value.
Practical tipStart with a high min_support (for example 1 to 5% of baskets on real data), filter on lift, sort by lift × support, and only then look at individual rules with a domain expert.

7. Strengths, limits and real-world use

StrengthsLimits
Simple, transparent: every rule is a sentence anyone can readCan output far too many rules; many are trivial or accidental
Needs no labels; finds patterns you did not think to look forOnly presence matters: no order, quantity, price or time
Works on huge transaction logs (FP-Growth)Shows co-occurrence, not cause and effect
Directly actionable: shelf layout, bundles, promotions, recommendationsRare-but-valuable items are lost at high support thresholds

🛒 Market-basket analysis

Shelf placement, bundle offers and coupons from what is bought together.

🎬 Recommendations

"People who watched X also watched Y": simple item-to-item recommenders can be built from rules.

🩺 Medical records

Which symptoms, drugs or conditions co-occur; spotting unexpected side-effect combinations for further study.

🌐 Web usage

Pages visited together in a session guide site layout and navigation links.

🏦 Cross-selling

Banks and insurers find which products customers hold together and offer the missing one.

🔎 Fraud and process mining

Frequent combinations of events in logs; unusual combinations stand out against the frequent ones.

Key takeaways

  • Association rules find "if X then Y" patterns in transaction data, with no labels.
  • Support = how common (baskets with both ÷ all baskets). Confidence = reliability (both ÷ baskets with X). Lift = confidence ÷ support(Y): above 1 = real association, 1 = independent, below 1 = they avoid each other. In our example bread → butter has support 0.60, confidence 0.86, lift 1.43, while milk → eggs has confidence 0.5 but lift 1.00.
  • Apriori finds the frequent itemsets level by level and uses the fact that every superset of an infrequent itemset is infrequent to skip most of the work (13 of 31 itemsets counted in our example). FP-Growth finds the same itemsets faster.
  • Rules come from splitting each frequent itemset in all ways and keeping those above a minimum confidence (and, better, a minimum lift).
  • Watch out for too many, trivial or accidental rules; association is not causation; validate on new data.
