Clustering Overview

So far every model had an answer key: a label to predict. In unsupervised learning there is no answer key. Clustering asks the data a different question: "Which of these things belong together?" This lesson covers what clustering is, how "similar" is measured, the main families of methods, and how to judge a result when nobody can tell you the right answer.

1. The big idea: finding groups without labels

In supervised learning each training row comes with a target (spam or not, house price). In unsupervised learning the rows have only features, and the algorithm looks for structure by itself. The main jobs are:

JobQuestion it answersLessons
ClusteringWhich rows form natural groups?this one, K-Means, DBSCAN, Hierarchical
Dimensionality reductionCan I describe the data with fewer columns?PCA, LDA
Association rulesWhich items occur together?Association Rule Learning
Anomaly detectionWhich rows do not fit at all?(a by-product of clustering such as DBSCAN's noise points)

Sorting a pile of unlabelled photos. Someone hands you 500 holiday photos with no captions and asks you to make piles. Nobody tells you the piles: you invent them. Photos of beaches go together, photos of food go together, and so on. You are using a sense of similarity, and you also decide how many piles to make. Two people may sort the same photos differently (by place, or by who is in them) and both can be "right". Clustering has exactly this character: a good result is one that is useful and consistent, not one that matches a hidden truth.

Formally, a cluster is a group of points that are similar to each other and dissimilar to points in other groups. Everything in this lesson follows from two choices: how to measure similarity, and what "a group" should look like.

2. What does "similar" mean? Distance

Almost every clustering algorithm turns similarity into a distance: small distance = similar. The most common:

DistanceIn wordsFor A = (1, 2) and B = (4, 6)
Euclideanthe straight-line distance: square the gaps, add, take the square root√(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5
Manhattanadd up the gaps, like walking along city blocks3 + 4 = 7
Cosineignore length, compare the direction of the two vectors (popular for text)angle between (1, 2) and (4, 6): cos ≈ 0.99, very similar direction

(You met Euclidean and Manhattan distance in the KNN lesson.) Distance-based clustering has the same catch as KNN: the features' units decide who wins.

By hand: why scaling matters

Three customers described by annual income in dollars and a spending score from 0 to 100:

CustomerIncome ($)Spending score
X40,00080
Y41,00030
Z52,00078

Raw distances. X to Y: √(1000² + 50²) = √2,502,500 = 1001.2. X to Z: √(12000² + 2²) = 12000.0. So by raw distance Y looks 12 times closer to X than Z does, even though X and Y are 50 points apart in spending, a huge gap, while X and Z spend almost identically (80 vs 78). The spending score has been drowned out: dollars are numerically so much bigger than score points that the 50-point gap barely registers.

After min-max scaling (subtract the smallest value and divide by the range, so each feature runs from 0 to 1): income (40,000 → 0, 41,000 → 0.083, 52,000 → 1) and score (30 → 0, 78 → 0.96, 80 → 1). Then X = (0, 1), Y = (0.083, 0), Z = (1, 0.96):
X to Y: √(0.083² + 1²) = 1.003. X to Z: √(1² + 0.04²) = 1.001.
Now both features count equally, and the two distances are about equal, as they should be: Y is far in spending, Z is far in income.

Rule: scale your features before distance-based clustering (StandardScaler or MinMaxScaler, from the Data Pre-processing lesson). Scaling does not decide which features matter; it just stops the units from deciding.

There are three real customer groups (differing in both income and spending). Without scaling, K-Means cuts the plot into vertical slices by income only. With scaling, it finds the true groups. The "agreement" number is the adjusted Rand index (ARI): 1.0 means the clusters match the hidden groups exactly, about 0 means they match no better than random. (You only have such hidden groups in demos; in real life you usually don't, see section 5.)

3. The families of clustering methods

Different algorithms have different ideas of what a "group" is. Each has its own lesson coming up; here is the map.

FamilyIdea of a clusterExampleGood atStruggles with
Centroid-basedpoints closest to a centre ("centroid")K-Meansround, similar-sized blobs; huge datasetscurved or elongated shapes, outliers, needs k
Density-baseda dense region separated from other dense regions by sparse spaceDBSCANany shape; finds outliers as "noise"; no k neededclusters of very different density; high dimensions
Hierarchicala tree: each point starts alone and the closest groups merge step by stepAgglomerative clusteringseeing structure at every level (the dendrogram); small dataslow on large data; merges can't be undone
Distribution-basedpoints drawn from overlapping bell-shaped (Gaussian) distributionsGaussian Mixture Modelsoverlapping, elliptical clusters; gives probabilities of membershipneeds assumptions about shape; can be unstable

Try the three families on four data shapes. Blobs are easy for everyone. Moons and rings have curved clusters. Stretched clusters are long and thin. (These are quick miniature implementations: K-Means with a smart start, hierarchical clustering with "single linkage", and DBSCAN; the following lessons explain each properly.)

  • Blobs + K-Means: perfect. This is the situation K-Means was designed for.
  • Moons or rings + K-Means: it cuts the shape in half with a straight boundary, because a K-Means cluster is always "the points nearest to one centre". Switch to DBSCAN or hierarchical (single link): they follow the curve.
  • DBSCAN's radius (eps): too small and everything becomes noise (grey ×); too large and separate groups merge into one. It is the one number you must tune.
  • Single-linkage hierarchical is happy with curves (moons and rings: perfect) but a single stray bridge of points can chain groups together. Try the stretched data: it scores about 0 because it peels off one or two stray points as tiny "clusters" and leaves everything else as one lump.
  • DBSCAN on the stretched stripes is sensitive: with the default eps it separates them on some random datasets and merges two stripes on others. Press "New random data" a few times and nudge eps. Any method that has one delicate setting needs that setting checked.
  • There is no "best" algorithm: the right choice depends on the shape you expect, the size of the data and whether you know k.

4. How many clusters, and is this clustering any good?

Because there are no labels, "accuracy" does not exist. We use internal measures that look only at the data and the clusters.

Inertia and the elbow

Inertia (also called within-cluster sum of squares, WCSS) adds up, for every point, the squared distance to its own cluster's centre. Tight clusters → small inertia. It always shrinks as you add clusters (with k = number of points it is 0), so you can't just minimise it. Instead, plot inertia against k and look for the elbow, where adding clusters stops helping much.

The silhouette score

The silhouette of one point compares two averages:

  • a = the average distance from the point to the other members of its own cluster (how snugly it fits in).
  • b = the average distance to the members of the nearest other cluster (how far the alternative is).
  • silhouette = (b − a) ÷ the larger of a and b, a number between −1 and +1: near +1 = clearly in the right cluster; near 0 = on the border; negative = probably in the wrong cluster.

The silhouette score of a clustering is the average over all points. Higher is better. A rough rule of thumb: above 0.7 strong structure, 0.5 to 0.7 reasonable, 0.25 to 0.5 weak, below 0.25 hardly any structure.

Play with both measures. The data always comes from 4 blobs; the question is whether the tools can tell:

Other ways to judge a clustering. (1) If you happen to have true labels (e.g. when benchmarking), compare with the adjusted Rand index or normalised mutual information. (2) Stability: cluster again on a random 80% of the data (or with different random starts); if the groups keep changing, they probably aren't real. (3) Usefulness: do the groups make sense to a domain expert? Do the "segments" behave differently on something you care about (revenue, churn)? A clustering that scores well but that nobody can act on has no value.

5. A practical workflow

  1. Clean and select features. Drop or encode text columns, handle missing values, remove features that carry no signal. Clustering is very sensitive to irrelevant features.
  2. Scale the features (StandardScaler is a good default).
  3. Pick an algorithm that matches the expected shape and size (section 3); start with K-Means as a baseline.
  4. Choose the settings (k, or eps and min_samples) using elbow, silhouette and stability.
  5. Profile the clusters: compute the average of each feature per cluster and give each cluster a human-readable name ("young high spenders", "cautious savers").
  6. Use them: as marketing segments, as a new feature in a supervised model, to compress data, or to spot outliers.

6. Clustering in scikit-learn

Every scikit-learn clusterer has fit/fit_predict and produces labels_. Our seven-point example first: the silhouettes match the hand calculation.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score, silhouette_samples

P = np.array([[1, 1], [2, 1], [1.5, 2], [4, 3], [6, 5], [7, 5], [6.5, 6]])
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(P)

print(km.labels_)                 # [0 0 0 0 1 1 1]
print(km.inertia_)                # 9.104...   (our 9.10)
print(km.cluster_centers_)        # [[2.125 1.75 ] [6.5 5.333]]
print(silhouette_samples(P, km.labels_).round(3))  # [0.728 0.737 0.727 0.117 0.791 0.82  0.817]
print(silhouette_score(P, km.labels_))             # 0.6768

Both tools point to k = 4, the true number of blobs. On messier real data they often disagree, which is why you combine them with stability and common sense. Next, scaling and shapes. In the first script the "true" groups differ in both income and score; K-Means is scored against them with the adjusted Rand index:

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score

rs = np.random.RandomState(0)
group = np.repeat([0, 1, 2], 50)
income = np.r_[rs.normal(40000, 6000, 50), rs.normal(52000, 6000, 50), rs.normal(64000, 6000, 50)]
score  = np.r_[rs.normal(25, 7, 50),       rs.normal(50, 7, 50),       rs.normal(75, 7, 50)]
C = np.c_[income, score]

raw    = KMeans(3, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(C)
scaled = KMeans(3, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(StandardScaler().fit_transform(C))
print(adjusted_rand_score(group, raw))      # 0.422   (income dominates)
print(adjusted_rand_score(group, scaled))   # 0.921   (both features used)

# shapes: two interleaved half-moons, ARI against the true moon labels
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN, AgglomerativeClustering
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
Xm, ym = make_moons(n_samples=300, noise=0.07, random_state=0)
print(adjusted_rand_score(ym, KMeans(2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(Xm)))             # 0.234
print(adjusted_rand_score(ym, DBSCAN(eps=0.2, min_samples=5).fit_predict(Xm)))                   # 1.0
print(adjusted_rand_score(ym, AgglomerativeClustering(2, linkage="single").fit_predict(Xm)))     # 1.0
print(adjusted_rand_score(ym, AgglomerativeClustering(2, linkage="ward").fit_predict(Xm)))       # 0.399

7. Strengths, limits and real-world use

StrengthsLimits
Needs no labels: works on data nobody has annotatedNo single right answer: results depend on the algorithm, features, scaling and k
Reveals structure and outliers you didn't know to look forHard to validate: the internal scores are hints, not proof
Cluster labels can become useful features for other modelsSensitive to irrelevant features and to units (scale!)
Fast baselines exist even for millions of rowsDistances become less meaningful in very high dimensions (see PCA)

🛍️ Customer segmentation

Group customers by behaviour, then design offers per segment. The classic use.

📰 Grouping documents

News articles or support tickets clustered by topic, so people can browse or route them.

🎨 Colour quantisation

Cluster the pixels of an image into 16 colours to shrink the file: each cluster centre becomes a palette colour.

🧬 Genes and cells

Cluster gene-expression profiles to find cell types or diseases with similar signatures.

🚨 Fraud and anomalies

Transactions that fall in no dense cluster are candidates for review.

🗺️ Geography

Cluster GPS pickups to find hot-spots for ride-share stations or store locations.

Key takeaways

  • Clustering = grouping unlabelled rows so that points in a group are similar and points in different groups are not. There is no answer key.
  • Similarity is a distance (Euclidean by default). Units decide the result, so scale first: in our example raw distances said 1001 vs 12000, scaled distances said 1.003 vs 1.001.
  • Families: centroid (K-Means), density (DBSCAN), hierarchical (agglomerative), distribution (Gaussian mixtures). Each assumes a different cluster shape: K-Means fails on curved clusters that DBSCAN handles.
  • Inertia always falls as k grows: look for the elbow. Silhouette = (b − a) ÷ max(a, b) per point (our example averages 0.68) and is highest for the best k.
  • Also judge by stability and usefulness: a cluster you can't name or act on is probably not worth keeping.

Mini task (20 minutes, optional).

  1. Compute the Euclidean and Manhattan distance between (2, 3) and (5, 7).
  2. Customers are described by age (20 to 60) and salary ($20,000 to $200,000). Which feature will dominate an unscaled distance, and what would you do about it?
  3. A cluster contains the one-dimensional points 1, 3 and 5. What is its centre and its inertia?
  4. A point has average distance a = 2 to its own cluster and b = 6 to the nearest other cluster. What is its silhouette? What if a = 5 and b = 4?
  5. Silhouette scores for k = 2, 3, 4 are 0.55, 0.71, 0.62. Which k do you pick, and what else would you check?
  6. Bonus: run the scaling code above with random_state=1 in RandomState and compare the two ARIs.
Show answers

1. Gaps are 3 and 4: Euclidean = √(9 + 16) = 5; Manhattan = 3 + 4 = 7.

2. Salary dominates: its range is thousands of times larger than age's. Standardise (or min-max scale) both features first.

3. Centre = (1 + 3 + 5) ÷ 3 = 3. Inertia = (1 − 3)² + (3 − 3)² + (5 − 3)² = 4 + 0 + 4 = 8.

4. (6 − 2) ÷ 6 = 0.67: well placed. With a = 5, b = 4: (4 − 5) ÷ 5 = −0.2: negative, so the point is probably in the wrong cluster (it is closer, on average, to the neighbouring one).

5. k = 3 (highest silhouette). Also check the inertia elbow, whether the clusters are stable when the data or the random start changes, and whether the three groups make sense to a domain expert.

6. Expect a big gap again: the raw run is dragged by income and scores well below the scaled run. In my check with seed 1 the two ARIs were about 0.557 (raw) and 0.901 (scaled); across seeds 0 to 3 the raw runs scored 0.42 to 0.56 and the scaled runs 0.83 to 0.92.
