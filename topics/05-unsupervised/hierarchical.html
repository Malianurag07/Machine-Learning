Hierarchical Clustering

Instead of committing to one number of clusters, hierarchical clustering builds a whole family tree of them. Start with every point alone, keep merging the two closest groups, and record every merge. The result, a tree called a dendrogram, lets you choose how many clusters you want after the fact, just by cutting it at a height.

1. The big idea: merge the closest, over and over

Building a family tree of languages, or companies merging. Imagine every small company in a city as its own group. Each year the two most similar (closest) companies merge into one. After a few years you have some mid-sized groups, and eventually a single giant company. If you wrote down which groups merged and how far apart they were when they did, you would have a complete history. Want to know the "natural groupings" at some level of detail? Go back to the moment when there were 3 companies left. Biologists build exactly such trees to classify species; the "tree of life" is a dendrogram.

Agglomerative clustering (bottom-up)

  1. Start with n clusters: every point is its own cluster.
  2. Find the two closest clusters and merge them into one.
  3. Repeat step 2 until only one cluster (containing everything) is left.
  4. Draw the merges as a tree: each merge is a horizontal bar whose height is the distance at which the merge happened.

(There is also a divisive version that goes top-down, splitting one big cluster repeatedly. It is rarer; scikit-learn and SciPy provide the agglomerative one, which is what "hierarchical clustering" normally means.)

No random start, no k to choose in advance: the tree is completely determined by the data, the distance measure and one more choice: how to measure the distance between two clusters.

2. Linkage: how far apart are two clusters?

Distance between two points is clear (Euclidean, as before). But once clusters contain several points, "the distance between cluster X and cluster Y" needs a rule, called the linkage:

LinkageDistance between two clusters =Tends to produce
Singlethe smallest distance between a point in one and a point in the other ("nearest neighbours")long, snake-like clusters; can follow curves; prone to chaining through a bridge of points
Completethe largest distance between any two points, one from each ("farthest neighbours")compact, similar-sized clusters; sensitive to outliers
Averagethe mean of all pairwise distances between the two clustersa compromise between single and complete
Wardhow much the total within-cluster squared spread (the inertia from K-Means) would increase if the two clusters were mergedcompact, roughly round and equal-sized clusters, like K-Means; often the best default

By hand: seven points on a line

Points at x = 0, 1, 2.1, 3.3, 4.6, 8, 8.5. Call them A, B, C, D, E, F, G. To feel the difference between linkages: A to E form a "chain" with gaps of about 1 to 1.3, and F, G are a close pair far away.

Single linkage.

  1. The closest pair of points is F and G (0.5): merge → {F,G} at height 0.5.
  2. Next closest: A and B (1.0) → {A,B} at 1.0.
  3. Now compare all cluster distances (the smallest gap between members). {A,B} to C: 2.1 − 1 = 1.1; C to D: 1.2; D to E: 1.3. The smallest is 1.1 → {A,B,C} at 1.1.
  4. {A,B,C} to D: 3.3 − 2.1 = 1.2 → {A,B,C,D} at 1.2.
  5. {A,B,C,D} to E: 4.6 − 3.3 = 1.3 → {A,…,E} at 1.3.
  6. Finally {A,…,E} to {F,G}: the nearest members are E and F: 8 − 4.6 = 3.4 → everything merged at 3.4.

Complete linkage (farthest members). Steps 1 and 2 are identical (0.5 and 1.0). Step 3: {A,B} to C is now the larger gap, 2.1 − 0 = 2.1; so the closest pair is C and D at 1.2 → {C,D}. Step 4: {C,D} to E: max(4.6 − 2.1, 4.6 − 3.3) = 2.5; {A,B} to {C,D}: 3.3 − 0 = 3.3; so → {C,D,E} at 2.5. Step 5: {A,B} to {C,D,E}: 4.6 − 0 = 4.6 → {A,…,E} at 4.6. Step 6: to {F,G}: 8.5 − 0 = 8.5 → 8.5.

Average linkage at step 4: {C,D} to E: average of 2.5 and 1.3 = 1.9. Step 5: {A,B} to {C,D,E}: the six distances are 2.1, 3.3, 4.6, 1.1, 2.3, 3.6, average = 17 ÷ 6 = 2.83. Step 6: the ten distances between {A,…,E} and {F,G} add up to 60.5, average = 6.05.

Ward linkage, step 4 in detail. Merging {C,D} (centre 2.7) with E (4.6): the increase in squared spread is (2 × 1 ÷ 3) × (4.6 − 2.7)² = 0.667 × 3.61 = 2.41. It is reported on the height scale as √(2 × 2.41) = 2.19 (that is the scale SciPy uses, chosen so that merging two single points gives their ordinary distance).

3. Reading the dendrogram and choosing the number of clusters

  • Leaves at the bottom are the individual points. Each U-shaped bar is a merge; its height is the distance between the two clusters when they merged.
  • Cut the tree with a horizontal line at some height. Every vertical line the cut crosses is one cluster (everything hanging below it). Cutting low gives many small clusters; cutting high gives a few large ones.
  • Long vertical lines mean "these groups were far apart when they finally merged", which suggests they are real, distinct clusters. A good cut is in the middle of the largest gap between successive merge heights.

Drag the cut height: with the Ward linkage on three blobs, the clusters below the cut stay coloured, and the number of clusters changes as you cross merge heights. The three natural clusters correspond to the big empty vertical stretch near the top.

Ways to decide where to cut: the largest-gap rule above; the silhouette score for each k (overview lesson); or a real-world requirement ("we need 5 customer segments"). On the iris flowers with Ward linkage, the last five merge heights are 3.83, 4.85, 6.4, 12.3 and 32.45 (I ran this): the biggest jump is at the very top (12.3 → 32.45, suggesting 2 clusters) and the next largest is 6.4 → 12.3 (suggesting 3, matching the three species).

4. Which linkage for which shape?

The same data, four linkages, and four kinds of shapes. The bridge data is two blobs joined by a thin line of points.

ShapeWorks wellFails
Round blobs, maybe unequal spreadWard (the most consistent in my tests); average and complete work on some datasetssingle (chains through the sparse region or peels off stray points: about 0 in the blobs demo on most seeds)
Curved shapes (moons, rings)SingleWard, complete, average: they cut the shapes into round chunks
Blobs joined by a thin bridge or with outliersWard, completesingle (merges everything through the bridge)

Numbers from scikit-learn (adjusted Rand index against the true groups, 1.0 = perfect). Two half-moons (300 points): single 1.0, average 0.52, Ward 0.40, complete 0.26. Three blobs of different spread (300 points): Ward 0.93, complete 0.74, single 0.57, average 0.57. Iris (three species): average 0.76, Ward 0.73, complete 0.64, single 0.56, where single linkage makes clusters of size 50, 2 and 98 (a typical chained result), while Ward gives 50, 36 and 64. No linkage wins everywhere: Ward is a good default for blob-like data, single linkage for curved structure.

5. Practical notes

  • Scale your features first: linkage relies on distances just like K-Means and DBSCAN.
  • Cost: the algorithm needs the distance between every pair of points, so memory and time grow at least with n². It is comfortable for thousands of points, painful for hundreds of thousands. For very large data use K-Means or MiniBatchKMeans first, then hierarchical clustering on the resulting centroids.
  • Merges are permanent: once two points are joined, a later, better idea can't separate them (unlike K-Means, which reassigns points each round). A bad early merge stays.
  • Deterministic: same data and settings give the same tree every time (no random starts).
  • Ward needs Euclidean distances. The other linkages accept other metrics (Manhattan, cosine, or a precomputed distance matrix).
  • Heat-map clustering: biology and finance often cluster both the rows and the columns of a table and display the dendrograms beside a colour-coded heat-map (for example seaborn's clustermap).

6. Hierarchical clustering in Python

SciPy builds the full merge table (and the dendrogram); scikit-learn's AgglomerativeClustering returns labels for a chosen k or cut height. Our seven points (SciPy's merge table reproduces the heights above):

import numpy as np
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, fcluster, dendrogram
from sklearn.cluster import AgglomerativeClustering

x = np.array([0, 1, 2.1, 3.3, 4.6, 8, 8.5]).reshape(-1, 1)

for method in ("single", "complete", "average", "ward"):
    Z = linkage(x, method=method)        # each row: [cluster a, cluster b, height, size of the new cluster]
    print(method, Z[:, 2].round(2))
# single   [0.5  1.   1.1  1.2  1.3  3.4 ]
# complete [0.5  1.   1.2  2.5  4.6  8.5 ]
# average  [0.5  1.   1.2  1.9  2.83 6.05]
# ward     [0.5  1.   1.2  2.19 4.39 10.23]

Z = linkage(x, method="single")
print(fcluster(Z, t=3, criterion="maxclust"))       # [2 2 2 2 3 1 1]  -> {A,B,C,D} {E} {F,G}
Z = linkage(x, method="complete")
print(fcluster(Z, t=3, criterion="maxclust"))       # [2 2 3 3 3 1 1]  -> {A,B} {C,D,E} {F,G}

print(AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=2, linkage="complete").fit_predict(x))   # [0 0 0 0 0 1 1]

# drawing the tree (needs matplotlib; I did not render it here):
#   import matplotlib.pyplot as plt; dendrogram(Z); plt.show()

Now on real data: iris, Ward linkage, cut by a distance threshold instead of a number of clusters. (Outputs are from an actual run with SciPy and scikit-learn 1.9.0; other versions may differ slightly.)

from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score

X, y = load_iris(return_X_y=True)

Z = linkage(X, method="ward")
print(Z[-5:, 2].round(2))                          # [ 3.83  4.85  6.4  12.3  32.45 ]  the top five merge heights
print(np.bincount(fcluster(Z, 3, "maxclust"))[1:]) # [50 36 64]   cluster sizes for k=3

model = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=None, distance_threshold=7, linkage="ward").fit(X)
print(model.n_clusters_)                           # 3   (a cut at height 7 crosses 3 vertical lines)

for method in ("single", "complete", "average", "ward"):
    labels = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=3, linkage=method).fit_predict(X)
    print(method, round(adjusted_rand_score(y, labels), 3))
# single 0.564 | complete 0.642 | average 0.759 | ward 0.731

7. Strengths, limits and real-world use

StrengthsLimits
No need to pick k up front: the dendrogram shows structure at every levelMemory and time grow roughly with n²: not for very large data
Deterministic and no random startsMerges can't be undone; sensitive to noise and outliers (depends on the linkage)
Different linkages suit different shapes; can follow curves (single)Each linkage has its own weakness: chaining (single), splitting big clusters (complete/Ward)
The tree is a natural picture for scientists (taxonomies, heat-maps)No predict for new points; choosing the cut still needs judgement

🧬 Genes and species

Cluster gene-expression profiles or DNA sequences and draw the tree next to a heat-map: the standard picture in bioinformatics and phylogenetics.

📰 Document taxonomies

Group articles or products into a hierarchy (topics, sub-topics), letting readers drill down.

🛍️ Customer segments at several levels

See both the 3 broad segments and the 12 finer ones from the same tree.

🖼️ Image segmentation

Merge neighbouring similar pixels or regions into objects, using the connectivity option.

🏦 Finance

Cluster assets by the correlation of their returns to build diversified portfolios: assets far apart in the tree diversify each other.

🔗 A step before other methods

Use the dendrogram to decide k, then run K-Means with that k.

Key takeaways

  • Agglomerative clustering starts with every point alone and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters; the merge history is the dendrogram. Cut it at a height to get any number of clusters.
  • The linkage defines "distance between clusters": single = nearest members, complete = farthest, average = mean of all pairs, Ward = smallest increase in within-cluster spread.
  • On our 7 points the merge heights were single 0.5/1.0/1.1/1.2/1.3/3.4, complete 0.5/1.0/1.2/2.5/4.6/8.5: single linkage chains along a line of points; complete and Ward form compact groups.
  • Choose the cut in the middle of the largest gap between merge heights, or with the silhouette. Ward is a good default for round blobs, single for curved shapes (but beware bridges and outliers).
  • Scale features first; it is deterministic but costs at least n² memory; merges are never undone.

1. First merge: 1 and 3 (distance 2) for every linkage, at height 2. Second merge (the cluster {1,3} with 8): single = smallest gap = 8 − 3 = 5; complete = largest gap = 8 − 1 = 7; average = (7 + 5) ÷ 2 = 6.

2. Distances between members: 1→6 = 5, 1→9 = 8, 2→6 = 4, 2→9 = 7. Single = smallest = 4; complete = largest = 8; average = (5 + 8 + 4 + 7) ÷ 4 = 6.
