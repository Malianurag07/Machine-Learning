K-Means Clustering

K-Means is the most widely used clustering algorithm, and it is delightfully simple: place k centres, let every point join its nearest centre, move each centre to the middle of its group, and repeat until nothing changes.

Beginner to intermediate≈ 85 min3 interactive demos

1. The big idea: k centres that keep moving

Opening k pizza shops. You want to serve a city with k = 3 shops. First you drop three shops at random spots. Every household orders from its nearest shop. Then each owner looks at the households they serve and moves the shop to the middle of those homes (the average location). Now some households are closer to a different shop, so they switch. Owners move again. After a few rounds nobody switches any more: each shop sits in the middle of its own neighbourhood. Those neighbourhoods are the clusters, and the shops are the centroids.

The algorithm (Lloyd's algorithm)

  1. Choose k, the number of clusters, and place k starting centroids (for example at k random data points).
  2. Assign: give every point to its nearest centroid (usual straight-line distance).
  3. Update: move each centroid to the mean of the points assigned to it (that is where the name "means" comes from).
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the assignments no longer change.

What is it trying to achieve? It minimises the inertia (also called within-cluster sum of squares): the sum, over all points, of the squared distance from the point to its own centroid. Small inertia = tight clusters.

Why do both steps lower the inertia? The assign step gives each point the centroid that is closest to it, which can only shrink its squared distance. The update step moves each centroid to the mean of its points, and the mean is the point that minimises the total squared distance to a group. Quick check on the one-dimensional group 1, 3, 5: the centre 3 (the mean) gives (1−3)² + (3−3)² + (5−3)² = 4 + 0 + 4 = 8; the centre 2 gives 1 + 1 + 9 = 11; the centre 4 gives 9 + 1 + 1 = 11. Since neither step can raise the inertia and there are only finitely many ways to assign points, the algorithm always stops. But it may stop at a local minimum rather than the best possible answer (section 4).

3. Choosing k

K-Means must be told k. Use the tools from the overview lesson: the elbow in the inertia curve and the silhouette score, plus domain knowledge ("marketing can only run four campaigns"). For the classic iris flower measurements, the inertia for k = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 is 152.4, 78.9, 57.2, 46.5, 39.0 (I ran this): it falls by 73 from k = 2 to 3, but only by 22, 11 and 7 afterwards, so the elbow is at about k = 3, matching the three species.

4. Where you start matters: local minima and k-means++

K-Means is guaranteed to stop, but not to find the best clustering. From an unlucky start it can settle in a local minimum: for example one centroid stranded between two blobs while another blob is shared by two centroids. Two standard remedies:

  • Run it several times from different starts and keep the run with the lowest inertia (n_init in scikit-learn).
  • Start smarter with k-means++: pick the first centroid at random; pick each further centroid at random too, but with probability proportional to the squared distance to the nearest centroid already chosen. Far-away points are much more likely to be picked, so the starting centres are spread out.

k-means++ by hand. Suppose the first centroid is A = (1, 1). Squared distances from A to the other points and the resulting chance of being picked as the second centroid:

On real data the same effect appears. On the iris data with k = 3 and only one start per run, I found (200 runs with scikit-learn 1.9.0): with random starting points 78.5% of the runs ended within 1% of the best inertia and 43 of 200 got stuck in a clearly worse solution (inertia about 143 to 146 instead of 78.9). With k-means++, all 200 reached the best solution. This is why scikit-learn uses k-means++ by default.

5. What K-Means cannot do well

A K-Means cluster is the set of points nearest to a centre, so clusters are always roughly round, similar in size, and separated by straight boundaries halfway between centres. Whenever real groups are not like that, K-Means struggles:

ProblemWhat happensWhat to do
Different sizes or spreadsBoundaries sit halfway between centres, so the wide cluster loses its edgesGaussian mixture models (they learn each cluster's own spread)
OutliersThe mean is dragged towards them; a centroid may be wasted on oneRemove or cap outliers first; use K-Medoids, or DBSCAN (labels them as noise)
Curved or elongated clustersCut across the shapeDBSCAN or hierarchical clustering; or transform the features first
Features in different unitsThe large-scale feature dominatesScale first (the overview lesson)
Categorical featuresThe mean of a category is meaninglessK-Modes, or one-hot encode with care
Many dimensionsAll distances look alike, clusters blurReduce dimensions first (PCA, coming up)
Huge datasetsEvery iteration touches every pointMiniBatchKMeans: updates from small random batches

6. K-Means in scikit-learn

Our seven points, starting from the same unlucky centroids, reproduce the hand calculation exactly:

import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

P = np.array([[1, 1], [1.5, 2], [3, 4], [5, 7], [3.5, 5], [4.5, 5], [3.5, 4.5]])

km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, init=np.array([[1, 1], [5, 7]]), n_init=1).fit(P)
print(km.labels_)             # [0 0 1 1 1 1 1]   -> {A, B} and {C, D, E, F, G}
print(km.cluster_centers_)    # [[1.25 1.5 ] [3.9  5.1 ]]
print(km.inertia_)            # 8.525
print(km.n_iter_)             # 3   (the algorithm stopped in the third iteration, as in our table)

print(km.predict([[2, 2], [4, 6]]))    # [0 1]     -> which cluster do new points fall in?
print(km.transform([[2, 2]]))          # [[0.901 3.636]]   -> distance to each centroid

Iris flowers, scaling and starting points (the outputs are from an actual run with scikit-learn 1.9.0; other versions may differ slightly):

from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score, silhouette_score

X, y = load_iris(return_X_y=True)

km = KMeans(n_clusters=3, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)
print(km.inertia_)                              # 78.85
print(adjusted_rand_score(y, km.labels_))       # 0.730   (agreement with the true species; the labels were NOT used to cluster)
print(silhouette_score(X, km.labels_))          # 0.553

Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
print(adjusted_rand_score(y, KMeans(3, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(Xs)))   # 0.620

for k in range(2, 7):
    print(k, KMeans(k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X).inertia_)
# 2 152.35 | 3 78.85 | 4 57.23 | 5 46.45 | 6 39.04     -> the elbow is around k = 3

Notice that scaling made the iris result worse here (ARI 0.73 → 0.62). All four iris measurements are in centimetres and of similar size, so scaling was not needed, and standardising boosts the influence of the least informative feature (sepal width). "Always scale" is a good default when units differ (as in the customer example), not a law.

K-Means is also used for colour quantisation: cluster an image's pixels (each pixel is a point in red-green-blue space) into k colours, and redraw every pixel with its centroid's colour.

from sklearn.datasets import load_sample_image

img = load_sample_image("china.jpg")                # 427 x 640 pixels, bundled with scikit-learn (needs Pillow)
pixels = (img / 255.0).reshape(-1, 3)
sample = pixels[np.random.RandomState(0).choice(len(pixels), 10000, replace=False)]   # fit on a sample: faster

for k in (4, 16, 64):
    q = KMeans(k, n_init=1, random_state=0).fit(sample)
    recon = q.cluster_centers_[q.predict(pixels)]   # every pixel replaced by its centroid colour
    print(k, np.mean((pixels - recon) ** 2))
# 4  colours: mean squared error 0.00702
# 16 colours: mean squared error 0.00180
# 64 colours: mean squared error 0.00060

The error keeps falling as k grows, as inertia always does. With 16 colours each pixel needs only 4 bits (log₂ 16) instead of 24, six times less, plus a tiny palette. (I measured the errors; I did not view the resulting pictures.)

7. Strengths, limits and real-world use

StrengthsLimits
Simple to explain, implement and use; a strong first baselineYou must choose k
Fast and scalable to millions of points (and MiniBatchKMeans for more)Assumes round, similar-sized clusters; sensitive to outliers and scaling
Centroids are easy to interpret ("the average customer in each segment")Can end in a local minimum; needs k-means++ and several restarts
Provides predict for new dataHard assignment only: no idea how sure a point's membership is

🛍️ Customer segmentation

Cluster customers on spend, frequency and recency, and profile each centroid as a segment.

🎨 Colour quantisation

Compress images or build colour palettes: the centroids are the palette.

📰 Grouping documents

Cluster text represented as numbers (such as TF-IDF vectors) to discover topics.

🎧 Vector quantisation

Audio and image codecs, and "visual vocabularies" in classic computer vision, store the nearest centroid instead of the raw vector.

📍 Facility placement

Where to place depots, cell towers or bike stations so that everyone is near one: the pizza-shop problem, literally.

🧩 Feature engineering

Use "which cluster" or "distance to each centroid" (transform) as extra features for a supervised model.

Key takeaways

  • K-Means repeats assign (each point to its nearest centroid) and update (each centroid to the mean of its points) until nothing changes.
  • It minimises the inertia, the total squared distance to the assigned centroid. In our example: 33.25 → 12.21 → 11.23 → 8.525 in three iterations.
  • It always stops, but possibly at a local minimum: use k-means++ starts (on iris, random starts got stuck in 43 of 200 runs, k-means++ in none) and several restarts.
  • Choose k with the elbow, the silhouette and domain knowledge. Inertia alone always prefers a larger k.
  • It assumes round, similar-sized clusters: it struggles with different sizes or spreads, outliers and curved or elongated shapes. Scale features whose units differ.

1. Point 2 is 1 from centroid 3 and 7 from 9 → centroid 3. Point 4: 1 from 3, 5 from 9 → centroid 3. Point 10: 7 from 3, 1 from 9 → centroid 9. New centroids: (2 + 4) ÷ 2 = 3 and 10. (The first centroid did not move; the second moved from 9 to 10. The next iteration changes nothing, so it stops.)

2. Centroid = (1 + 2 + 6) ÷ 3 = 3. Inertia = (1−3)² + (2−3)² + (6−3)² = 4 + 1 + 9 = 14.

3. Inertia never increases as k grows, and reaches 0 when k equals the number of points (every point its own cluster). So it always "prefers" more clusters; you must look for the elbow or use another measure such as the silhouette.

4. The total is 1 + 4 + 25 = 30, so the chances are 1 ÷ 30 = 3.3%, 4 ÷ 30 = 13.3% and 25 ÷ 30 = 83.3%.
