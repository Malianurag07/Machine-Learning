Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)

PCA looks for the direction where the data varies the most. LDA looks for the direction where the classes are best separated. It uses the labels to squeeze many columns into a few, keeping exactly the information that tells the classes apart. It is also a simple, fast classifier in its own right.

Intermediate≈ 85 min3 interactive demos

Where does it belong? LDA sits next to PCA in most courses (this syllabus lists it in the same block) because both reduce dimensions. But unlike PCA and clustering it is supervised: it needs a class label for every training row.

1. The big idea: look from where the classes separate

Two singers, one microphone. Two people are singing in a room and you can place a single microphone anywhere. If you put it where the loudest overall sound comes from (PCA's idea), you might capture a blend of both voices. What you want is the position where the two voices are easiest to tell apart, which may be a quiet spot where one voice dominates on each side. LDA finds that position. PCA answers "where is there the most going on?", LDA answers "where do the groups differ the most, compared with how much each group wanders on its own?"

Here is the situation where the two goals disagree. Two classes form parallel elongated clouds, shifted slightly across their long direction. The direction of greatest spread is along the clouds, where the two classes overlap completely. The useful direction is across them.

Rotate the axis by hand. The coloured histograms show how the two classes look when every point is projected onto the axis (a 1-D view): where they overlap heavily you cannot tell them apart. The score J at the bottom right measures the separation; its peak is the LDA direction, while PCA's direction sits in a valley.

2. The criterion: far apart, but tight inside

For a direction, project every point onto it and look at the two classes as 1-D clouds. A good direction has two properties at once:

  • the class averages are far apart (large "between-class" gap), and
  • each class is tightly bunched around its own average (small "within-class" spread).

Fisher's criterion combines both into one score:

J = (gap between the two class averages)² ÷ (spread of class 1 + spread of class 2)

where "spread" is the sum of squared distances from each point to its class average. LDA picks the direction with the largest J. For two classes there is a closed-form answer, worked out below.

By hand: two classes, five points each

Class A(0, 2)(1, 4)(2, 3)(3, 6)(4, 5)
Class B(2, 0)(3, 2)(4, 1)(5, 4)(6, 3)

1. Class averages. A: (2, 4). B: (4, 2). The difference B − A is Δ = (2, −2).

2. Within-class scatter matrix. For each class, sum the products of the deviations from that class's average. Class A's deviations are (−2, −2), (−1, 0), (0, −1), (1, 2), (2, 1): the sums give xx = 4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4 = 10, yy = 10, xy = 4 + 0 + 0 + 2 + 2 = 8, so [[10, 8], [8, 10]]. Class B has the same deviations, so the pooled within-class scatter is Sw = [[20, 16], [16, 20]]. Both clouds are long diagonal streaks (the x and y values rise together).

3. Fisher's direction is w = Sw−1 × Δ. In words: take the direction between the class averages, and then "un-stretch" it by the shape of the clouds, so we do not favour directions along which the clouds are naturally long. The determinant of Sw is 20 × 20 − 16 × 16 = 144, and the inverse is (1 ÷ 144) × [[20, −16], [−16, 20]]. Then w = (1 ÷ 144) × (20 × 2 + (−16) × (−2), −16 × 2 + 20 × (−2)) = (1 ÷ 144) × (72, −72) = (0.5, −0.5). That points along (1, −1): across the diagonal streaks.

4. Project. Using the unit vector (1, −1) ÷ √2, the score of a point is (x − y) ÷ 1.414:

Class A−1.41−2.12−0.71−2.12−0.71
Class B+1.41+0.71+2.12+0.71+2.12

All of class A is below −0.7 and all of class B is above +0.7: perfect separation with a cut at 0. The projected averages are −1.41 and +1.41 (a gap of 2.83), and each class's spread is 0² + 0.71² + 0.71² + 0.71² + 0.71² = 2.0. So J = 2.83² ÷ (2 + 2) = 8 ÷ 4 = 2.0.

5. Compare with PCA. The total scatter of all ten points is Sw + Sb = [[20, 16], [16, 20]] + [[10, −10], [−10, 10]] = [[30, 6], [6, 30]], whose biggest axis is the diagonal (1, 1) (eigenvalue 36 versus 24 for (1, −1)). So PC1 = (1, 1) ÷ √2, the long direction of both streaks, and it explains 60% of the variance. Projecting onto PC1 gives class A the scores −2.83, −0.71, −0.71, 2.12, 2.12 and class B exactly the same five numbers: the classes are completely on top of each other and J = 0.

PCA would happily keep the useless direction and throw away the one that matters. LDA does the opposite. A new point such as (5, 3) has score (5 − 3) ÷ 1.414 = +1.41: class B.

3. More than two classes

With C classes the same idea gives several directions. Two matrices describe the data:

  • Within-class scatter Sw: how spread out each class is around its own average, all classes added up (the "noise").
  • Between-class scatter Sb: how far the class averages are from the overall average, weighted by class size (the "signal").

The LDA directions are the eigenvectors of Sw−1 Sb, ordered by eigenvalue: the first direction has the best signal-to-noise ratio, the second is next best, and so on. Because C class averages can only span C − 1 dimensions, there are at most C − 1 useful directions (and never more than the number of features). Three iris species → at most 2 discriminants; ten digits → at most 9. (scikit-learn raises an error if you ask for more.) Each direction's eigenvalue, as a share of the total, is its explained variance ratio: on iris LDA gives 99.1% to LD1 and 0.9% to LD2.

LDA does not care about units. Rescaling a feature (from kilometres to metres, or standardising) rescales the within-class and between-class scatter in a matching way, so LDA's answer does not change (I checked: the correlation between the LDA scores on raw and standardised wine data is 1.0000). PCA does change (see the last lesson). This makes LDA convenient, but scaling is still useful if you follow it with a distance-based model such as KNN.

4. LDA as a classifier

LDA can also predict classes directly. It assumes:

  1. each class's points follow a bell-shaped (Gaussian) cloud, and
  2. all the classes share the same shape (the same covariance: same size, same tilt of the cloud), with only the centres different.

Given those assumptions, Bayes' rule says: classify a new point into the class with the highest (how well the point fits that class's cloud) × (how common that class is, its prior). Because the shapes are equal, the boundary between any two classes turns out to be a straight line (a flat plane in more dimensions), which is why it is called linear discriminant analysis. If the classes are allowed different shapes, the boundary curves and the method is called QDA (quadratic discriminant analysis).

  • With "same shape" data LDA's straight line is right, and QDA gains nothing.
  • With "different shapes" data (one tight class inside a wider one), LDA's straight line struggles and QDA's curved boundary does better. In a scikit-learn experiment with 300 points per class (a similar setup) 5-fold accuracy was 0.567 for LDA and 0.707 for QDA.
  • Move the prior of the orange class: the boundary shifts towards the less likely class, which is how you tell the model "orange is rare, so don't predict it without strong evidence". (By default scikit-learn takes the priors from the class frequencies in the training data.)

LDA vs logistic regression: both give linear boundaries. LDA is a bit more efficient when its Gaussian, equal-shape assumptions hold and the sample is small; logistic regression makes fewer assumptions and is more robust when they fail. It is often worth trying both.

Many features, few rows: LDA needs to estimate the within-class covariance matrix, and with more features than samples it is badly behaved (it cannot be inverted). Shrinkage blends the estimate with a simple diagonal matrix. In a synthetic test with 60 rows and 100 features, plain LDA scored 0.70 in cross-validation and LDA with shrinkage="auto" scored 0.817 (one dataset, so take it as an illustration).

5. LDA in scikit-learn

Our ten points reproduce the hand calculation (scikit-learn scales the axis so the pooled within-class variance of the scores is 1, and it may flip the sign; the direction is the same):

import numpy as np
from sklearn.discriminant_analysis import LinearDiscriminantAnalysis as LDA
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

X = np.array([[0, 2], [1, 4], [2, 3], [3, 6], [4, 5],       # class A
              [2, 0], [3, 2], [4, 1], [5, 4], [6, 3]], float)   # class B
y = np.array([0] * 5 + [1] * 5)

lda = LDA(solver="eigen", n_components=1).fit(X, y)
print(lda.means_)                        # [[2. 4.] [4. 2.]]     the class averages
print(lda.scalings_[:, 0])               # [-1.118  1.118]       the direction (-1, 1) = our (1, -1), sign flipped
print(lda.transform(X).ravel().round(2)) # [ 2.24  3.35  1.12  3.35  1.12 -2.24 -1.12 -3.35 -1.12 -3.35]   perfectly separated at 0
print(lda.score(X, y))                   # 1.0

pc1 = PCA(1).fit(X)
print(pc1.components_)                   # [[0.7071 0.7071]]     the diagonal: useless for the classes
z = pc1.transform(X).ravel()
print(z[:5].round(2), z[5:].round(2))    # both classes: [-2.83 -0.71 -0.71  2.12  2.12]  identical!

Real data. All figures below are from an actual run with scikit-learn 1.9.0 (5-fold stratified cross-validation, shuffled with a fixed seed; other versions or seeds will differ slightly):

from sklearn.datasets import load_iris, load_wine, load_digits
from sklearn.discriminant_analysis import QuadraticDiscriminantAnalysis as QDA
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, StratifiedKFold
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

cv = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
Xi, yi = load_iris(return_X_y=True)
Xw, yw = load_wine(return_X_y=True)

print(LDA(solver="eigen").fit(Xi, yi).explained_variance_ratio_)     # [0.9912 0.0088]   iris: two discriminants
print(LDA(solver="eigen").fit(Xw, yw).explained_variance_ratio_)     # [0.6875 0.3125]   wine

# LDA and QDA as classifiers
for name, (X_, y_) in {"iris": (Xi, yi), "wine": (Xw, yw)}.items():
    print(name, cross_val_score(LDA(), X_, y_, cv=cv).mean().round(4), cross_val_score(QDA(), X_, y_, cv=cv).mean().round(4))
# iris  LDA 0.98    QDA 0.9667
# wine  LDA 0.9943  QDA 0.9886

# as a dimensionality reducer in front of KNN: 13 features -> 2, inside the pipeline so it is fitted on training folds only
print(cross_val_score(make_pipeline(LDA(n_components=2), KNeighborsClassifier()), Xw, yw, cv=cv).mean())                        # 0.9887
print(cross_val_score(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(2), KNeighborsClassifier()), Xw, yw, cv=cv).mean())                  # 0.9663
print(cross_val_score(make_pipeline(PCA(2), KNeighborsClassifier()), Xw, yw, cv=cv).mean())                                    # 0.6579  (unscaled PCA: proline dominates)

Xd, yd = load_digits(return_X_y=True)                               # 64 pixels, 10 digits -> at most 9 discriminants
print(cross_val_score(make_pipeline(LDA(n_components=9), KNeighborsClassifier()), Xd, yd, cv=cv).mean())                       # 0.9661
print(cross_val_score(LDA(), Xd, yd, cv=cv).mean())                                                                            # 0.951

LDA(n_components=3).fit(Xi, yi)      # ValueError: n_components cannot be larger than min(n_features, n_classes - 1)

Reading the numbers: with two components, LDA (98.9%) beat scaled PCA (96.6%) on wine, and beat unscaled PCA by a huge margin (65.8%). On iris the unscaled PCA does very well too (96%; iris's classes happen to differ along its high-variance directions), while scaled PCA scores 90% and LDA 96%. On the digits, two components of either method are weak (about 62% for PCA and 65% for LDA), but nine LDA components with KNN reach 96.6%. LDA is best when the classes really are separated by a few directions; it is not magic.

6. Strengths, limits and real-world use

StrengthsLimits
Uses the labels, so it keeps exactly the information that separates classesNeeds labels; at most C − 1 dimensions
Fast, simple, no hyper-parameters to tune (apart from shrinkage); scale-invariantAssumes roughly Gaussian classes with equal covariance; fails on curved or multi-lobed classes
Also a probabilistic classifier, good with small samplesAffected by outliers; unstable with more features than rows (unless shrunk)
Gives an interpretable picture: which combination of features separates the groupsCan only find linear combinations; not effective if the classes differ in spread but not in centre

😀 Face recognition

"Fisherfaces": reduce images with PCA, then LDA, to find directions that distinguish people rather than lighting.

🩺 Medical diagnosis

Combine several lab measurements into one discriminant score separating patient groups; the weights are interpretable.

💳 Credit scoring (historically)

Altman's Z-score for predicting bankruptcy was one of the best-known applications of discriminant analysis.

🌿 Ecology and species classification

Fisher's original iris example: separate species from flower measurements.

📊 Pre-processing for other classifiers

Reduce to C − 1 discriminants before KNN or a tree, and to plot the classes.

🧠 Brain-signal decoding

Regularised LDA is a standard, fast classifier for EEG signals in brain-computer interfaces.

Key takeaways

  • PCA maximises total variance and ignores labels; LDA maximises class separation and needs labels. They can pick very different axes: in our example PCA's axis gave J = 0 (classes identical) and LDA's gave J = 2.0 (perfect).
  • Fisher's criterion: J = (gap between class averages)² ÷ (within-class spread). The best direction for two classes is w = Sw−1 (μB − μA): in our example w ∝ (1, −1).
  • With C classes there are at most C − 1 discriminant axes (eigenvectors of Sw−1Sb), and never more than the number of features.
  • LDA is unaffected by rescaling features; PCA is affected.
  • As a classifier, LDA assumes Gaussian classes with a shared covariance and gives linear boundaries; QDA allows different covariances (curved boundaries); shrinkage helps with many features and few rows; the priors shift the boundary.
  • Put it inside a Pipeline when you use it before another model, so it is fitted on training folds only.

1. Δ = (5 − 1, 2 − 2) = (4, 0). Sw−1 = [[0.5, 0], [0, 0.5]], so w = (2, 0): the x direction. That makes sense: the classes differ only in x, and the clouds are round (no stretch to correct for), so the best axis is simply the line joining the two averages.
