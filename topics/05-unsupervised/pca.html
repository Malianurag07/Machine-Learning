Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Real datasets often have dozens or hundreds of columns, but many of them move together and repeat the same information. PCA finds a few new columns, each a blend of the old ones, that capture as much of the variation in the data as possible. You keep the few most informative ones and drop the rest.

1. The big idea: the best angle to look from

Photographing a sculpture. A statue is three-dimensional, but a photo is flat. Some camera angles are terrible: from directly behind, or edge-on, you lose most of the shape. From the best angle, you see nearly everything in a single flat picture. PCA is a way of finding that best angle for data: given points in many dimensions, it finds the flat view (2-D, or 3-D, or whatever you ask for) that keeps as much of the spread of the data as possible.

Why would you want fewer columns?

  • Seeing the data. You cannot plot 30 dimensions, but you can plot 2.
  • Removing redundancy. If height in centimetres and height in inches are both columns, one is enough. Real data has softer versions of the same thing (income and spending, petal length and petal width).
  • Removing noise. The directions with tiny variation are often mostly noise; dropping them cleans the data.
  • Speed and stability. Fewer columns make later models faster and, in some cases, less prone to overfitting. Correlated columns can also make linear models unstable (multicollinearity); PCA's new columns are uncorrelated.

PCA is unsupervised: it never looks at labels, only at how the columns vary together.

2. What PCA actually does

PCA invents a new set of axes, called principal components:

  • PC1 is the direction along which the data is most spread out.
  • PC2 is the direction, at a right angle to PC1, with the most spread that is left. PC3 is at right angles to both, and so on.
  • Each component is a weighted blend of the original columns (for example, "0.7 × height + 0.7 × weight").
  • The components come out in order of importance: PC1 has the most variance, and each later one has less.

To reduce dimensions, keep the first k components and describe every point by its k scores (its coordinates along those axes). Here is the key picture: a line through the data cloud. Rotate the line, and see two things change at once.

Each point is dropped onto the line (the red segments). The blue curve, the spread of the shadow along the line, peaks at one angle. The red curve, the information lost (the squared lengths of the red segments), is at its lowest at the very same angle, because the two always add up to the same total. So "keep the most variance" and "lose the least information" are the same goal. The angle at the peak is PC1.

3. The recipe, with the maths in plain words

  1. Centre the data: subtract each column's average, so the cloud is centred on the origin. (If the columns have different units, also divide by each column's standard deviation: standardise. See section 6.)
  2. Measure how the columns vary together with the covariance matrix: variances of each column on the diagonal, and covariances (how much two columns move together) off the diagonal.
  3. Find its eigenvectors and eigenvalues. In words: the covariance matrix describes an ellipse-shaped cloud. The eigenvectors are the ellipse's axes (long axis, short axis, ...): these are the principal components. Each eigenvalue is the variance of the data along its axis.
  4. Sort the components by eigenvalue, largest first.
  5. Project: a point's score on a component is the dot product of the (centred) point with that eigenvector. Keep the first k scores.

How much did we keep? The explained variance ratio of a component is its eigenvalue divided by the sum of all the eigenvalues. Adding the first k ratios tells you what fraction of the total variation the k-dimensional summary retains.

variance of x = (4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4) ÷ 4 = 2.5; variance of y = (4 + 0 + 1 + 4 + 1) ÷ 4 = 2.5; covariance = (dx·dy summed) ÷ 4 = (4 + 0 + 0 + 2 + 2) ÷ 4 = 2.
So the matrix is [[2.5, 2], [2, 2.5]]: x and y both have spread 2.5 and move together strongly.

3. Eigenvalues. For a 2×2 matrix of the form [[a, b], [b, a]] the eigenvalues are simply a + b and a − b (because the two mixed directions (1, 1) and (1, −1) are its axes): λ₁ = 2.5 + 2 = 4.5 and λ₂ = 2.5 − 2 = 0.5. The eigenvectors (each scaled to length 1): PC1 = (0.707, 0.707), the diagonal direction; PC2 = (−0.707, 0.707), the perpendicular direction. (The sign of an eigenvector is arbitrary: (−0.707, −0.707) describes the same axis.)

4. Explained variance. Total = 4.5 + 0.5 = 5 (equal to the sum of the two original variances, 2.5 + 2.5: nothing is created or lost, only redistributed). PC1 explains 4.5 ÷ 5 = 90%, PC2 explains 10%.

6. Keep only PC1 (2-D → 1-D). Each point is described by one number. To see what we lost, rebuild the points from their PC1 score: mean + score × PC1 = (3, 3) + score × (0.707, 0.707). Point a: score −2.83 → (1, 1). Point b: −0.71 → (2.5, 2.5). Point c → (2.5, 2.5). Point d: 2.12 → (4.5, 4.5); e → (4.5, 4.5). The rebuilt points all lie on the line y = x. The information lost is the sum of squared PC2 scores = 0 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 = 2.0, which is 10% of the total squared spread (4 × 5 = 20), matching the 10% left unexplained.

So one number per point instead of two, and 90% of the variation remains. Now the same idea in 3-D: the points below form a tilted "pancake". From most angles it looks like a messy blob; from the right angle it is a clean flat sheet.

4. How many components to keep?

Look at the explained variance ratios, drawn as a scree plot (bars) with the running total (line). Common rules:

  • Keep enough components to reach a target of cumulative variance, often 90 to 95% (in scikit-learn: PCA(n_components=0.95)).
  • Look for the elbow, where the bars stop dropping steeply and flatten out.
  • Or choose by what works: try k = 2, 5, 10 ... inside a cross-validated pipeline and pick the smallest k that keeps the model's accuracy.
  • For visualisation, simply take 2 (or 3), and report how much variance they represent.

For the iris flowers (4 features), the first two components explain 97.8% of the variance (92.5% + 5.3%): two numbers per flower are almost as good as four, and the three species are clearly visible. The wine data has 13 features; standardised, PC1 explains 36.2% and the first two 55.4%, so a 2-D picture keeps only about half the variation (5 components for 80%, 10 for 95%). PCA compresses well when the columns are strongly related and less well when they are not.

5. Reading the components: loadings

Each component is a list of weights, one per original column, called its loadings. A large weight (positive or negative) means that column matters for that component. For iris (in centimetres, unscaled) PC1 = (0.36, −0.09, 0.86, 0.36) on (sepal length, sepal width, petal length, petal width): PC1 is essentially "overall flower size, dominated by petal length". PC2 = (0.66, 0.73, −0.17, −0.08) mostly contrasts the sepal measurements. That is what makes PCA interpretable when it works, but components are often mixtures with no clean name. Signs are arbitrary: flipping a whole component changes nothing.

6. The scaling trap

PCA follows variance, and variance depends on units. A column measured in the thousands has a huge variance, so it dominates PC1 regardless of whether it is important. On the wine data (13 chemical measurements), without scaling PC1 explains 99.8% of the variance, and its loading on the column "proline" is 1.00 (all other weights are almost 0): proline is simply measured in the hundreds and thousands, while the other columns are mostly between 0 and about 30 (magnesium reaches about 160, still far smaller). After standardising every column (mean 0, standard deviation 1), PC1 explains 36.2% and blends many measurements. Standardise your features first unless they are all in the same units and you want the units' natural scale to count (as with iris, where all four are centimetres and scaling changes little).

Another good habit: fit PCA on the training data only and then apply it to the test data, exactly as with scalers. Put the scaler and PCA inside a Pipeline so this happens automatically inside cross-validation.

7. PCA in scikit-learn

Our five points reproduce the hand calculation:

import numpy as np
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

X = np.array([[1, 1], [2, 3], [3, 2], [4, 5], [5, 4]], dtype=float)

pca = PCA(n_components=2).fit(X)
print(pca.mean_)                        # [3. 3.]
print(pca.components_)                  # [[ 0.7071  0.7071] [-0.7071  0.7071]]   -> PC1, PC2
print(pca.explained_variance_)          # [4.5 0.5]      -> the eigenvalues
print(pca.explained_variance_ratio_)    # [0.9 0.1]
print(pca.transform(X).round(2))        # the scores:  [[-2.83  0.] [-0.71  0.71] [-0.71 -0.71] [ 2.12  0.71] [ 2.12 -0.71]]

pca1 = PCA(n_components=1).fit(X)
X_back = pca1.inverse_transform(pca1.transform(X))
print(X_back)                           # [[1. 1.] [2.5 2.5] [2.5 2.5] [4.5 4.5] [4.5 4.5]]   -> our reconstruction
print(((X - X_back) ** 2).sum())        # 2.0

Iris and wine (the outputs are from an actual run with scikit-learn 1.9.0; other versions may differ slightly):

from sklearn.datasets import load_iris, load_wine
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

Xi, yi = load_iris(return_X_y=True)
print(PCA().fit(Xi).explained_variance_ratio_.round(4))                             # [0.9246 0.0531 0.0171 0.0052]
print(PCA().fit(StandardScaler().fit_transform(Xi)).explained_variance_ratio_.round(4))   # [0.7296 0.2285 0.0367 0.0052]

Xw, yw = load_wine(return_X_y=True)
print(PCA().fit(Xw).explained_variance_ratio_[:2])                                  # [0.9981 0.0017]   -> dominated by proline!
ratios = PCA().fit(StandardScaler().fit_transform(Xw)).explained_variance_ratio_
print(ratios[:3].round(3), ratios.cumsum()[[1, 4, 9]].round(3))                     # [0.362 0.192 0.111]  [0.554 0.802 0.962]

A more realistic use: 8×8-pixel images of handwritten digits (64 features). How many components keep 95% of the variance, and how well does a classifier do on the compressed data?

from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

Xd, yd = load_digits(return_X_y=True)
print(PCA(n_components=0.95).fit(Xd).n_components_)     # 29   (64 features -> 29 keep 95% of the variance; 13 keep 80%, 21 keep 90%, 41 keep 99%)

for k in (2, 5, 10, 20, 30):
    pipe = make_pipeline(PCA(n_components=k), KNeighborsClassifier())    # PCA is re-fitted inside every CV fold
    print(k, cross_val_score(pipe, Xd, yd, cv=5).mean().round(4))
# 2 0.5949 | 5 0.8837 | 10 0.9405 | 20 0.9583 | 30 0.9616
print(cross_val_score(KNeighborsClassifier(), Xd, yd, cv=5).mean().round(4))   # 0.9627   with all 64 pixels

Twenty components (less than a third of the columns) give 95.8% accuracy versus 96.3% for all 64 pixels; two components (28.5% of the variance) give only 59.5%, enough for a picture but not for recognising digits. I also tried denoising: after adding random noise to every pixel, keeping the components that explain 80% of the variance (37 of them) cut the average squared error against the clean images from 15.9 to 10.4. That is a real but moderate improvement, not magic.

8. Limits, alternatives and real-world use

StrengthsLimits
Simple, fast, deterministic, and works on any numeric tableLinear only: it cannot unroll a curved surface (a spiral, a Swiss roll)
Uncorrelated new columns; controllable information lossBig variance is not the same as usefulness: a low-variance direction can be the one that separates the classes (see LDA, next)
Great for plotting, compression and denoisingSensitive to scaling and outliers; components can be hard to interpret
Can be reversed approximately (inverse_transform)New columns are blends of all old ones, so you lose the original meaning of each column

Alternatives: LDA (next lesson) finds directions that separate known classes, using the labels. t-SNE and UMAP make good 2-D pictures of non-linear structure but are for visualisation only, and their distances are not to be trusted. Autoencoders (neural networks) can compress non-linearly. Feature selection keeps a subset of the original columns unchanged, which keeps meaning but not blended information.

📊 Visualising big data

Project 30 to 1000 features onto PC1 × PC2 to see clusters, outliers and batches in one plot.

🧬 Genetics

PCA of genetic variation across thousands of markers famously reproduces the geographic map of a population.

🖼️ Image compression and eigenfaces

Faces or digits described by a few dozen components instead of thousands of pixels.

📈 Finance

Hundreds of correlated stock or bond series are driven by a handful of factors (the market, interest rates ...) that PCA extracts.

⚙️ Preprocessing

Decorrelate and shrink the inputs before KNN, clustering, or regression to speed up and stabilise them.

🎧 Noise reduction

Sensor and signal data: keep the components with real signal and drop the ones that are mostly noise.

Key takeaways

  • PCA finds new perpendicular axes (principal components) ordered by how much variance they capture; you keep the first k and drop the rest.
  • Recipe: centre (and usually standardise) → covariance matrix → eigenvectors (the axes) and eigenvalues (variance along each) → project.
  • Maximising the variance kept and minimising the squared error lost are the same thing. In our example: eigenvalues 4.5 and 0.5, so PC1 keeps 90%, and the reconstruction error from dropping PC2 is 2.0.
  • Choose k with the explained variance ratio (for example 95%), the scree elbow, or cross-validation. Digits: 29 of 64 components keep 95% of the variance.
  • Scale first: unscaled wine has PC1 = 99.8% just because proline is measured in the thousands.
  • Linear, unsupervised, and blind to class labels: high variance does not always mean useful for prediction. Fit it inside a pipeline, on the training data only.
