Label Spreading

Label Propagation treats every labelled point as sacred: its label can never change, even if it is wrong. Label Spreading relaxes this. Labelled points keep most of their label but are allowed to be influenced by their neighbours, so the method tolerates noise in the labels and is often a little more accurate. One extra number, α, controls the balance.

1. The big idea: labelled points that can be persuaded

Stubborn taps versus open-minded people. In the last lesson, labelled points were taps pouring dye at a fixed rate, forever, whatever happened around them. Now picture instead a room of people, some of whom were told an answer ("you are a red person"). Each round, everyone does two things: they keep a share of what they were originally told (weight 1 − α), and they take in the average opinion of their neighbours (weight α). A person told "red" who is surrounded by "blue" friends will drift towards blue over the rounds. If α is small, people mostly stick to their original information; if α is large, they follow the crowd.

2. The algorithm

  1. Build the similarity graph exactly as before: weights W between points (RBF kernel e−γ d² or k nearest neighbours). Let di be the total weight of point i (its degree).
  2. Normalise symmetrically: Sij = Wij ÷ √(di × dj). In words: the influence between two points is reduced when either of them is very "popular" (has many strong links), so hubs don't dominate.
  3. Start with F = Y0, where Y0 holds 1 at the given class of each labelled point and zeros for everything else.
  4. Repeat: new F = α × (S × F) + (1 − α) × Y0. Every point's new label distribution is α × (the weighted average of its neighbours' distributions) plus (1 − α) × its original label information.
  5. Predict: the class with the largest value in a point's row of F.

Because of the (1 − α) × Y0 term the original labels are re-injected every round but never forced. And the repetition always converges to one answer, which can also be written in one step as F* = (1 − α) × (I − α S)−1 × Y0.

For the curious: what is it optimising? The result is the labelling that best balances two wishes: smoothness (points joined by a strong link should get similar values) and fit (points should stay close to the labels they were given). α sets the exchange rate between the two: a larger α means more weight on smoothness. Label Propagation is the extreme case in which the fit to the given labels is absolute.

By hand: the same four-point chain

The chain from the last lesson: A — (2) — P — (1) — Q — (1) — B, with A labelled class A and B labelled class B. The total weights (degrees) are: A 2, P 3, Q 2, B 1. So:

3. Robustness to wrong labels

Real labels contain mistakes. Take a chain of five equally linked points where the two ends are labelled class 0, and the middle point was labelled class 1 by mistake. The class-0 value each point ends up with (from scikit-learn) for several α:

With Label Propagation the middle label is fixed and stays wrong. With Label Spreading the two correct neighbours pull on it, and if α is large enough (here above about 0.9) they win. Note that the price of a large α is that the given labels are trusted less and less: at α = 0.99 the values are all close to 0.6, barely distinguishing the points.

4. Label Spreading in scikit-learn

scikit-learn's LabelSpreading has the same interface as LabelPropagation plus alpha. To check that it does what we computed by hand, we can even hand it our chain through a custom kernel (a function that returns our weight matrix):

import numpy as np
from sklearn.semi_supervised import LabelSpreading

W = np.array([[0, 2, 0, 0], [2, 0, 1, 0], [0, 1, 0, 1], [0, 0, 1, 0]], float)     # A -2- P -1- Q -1- B
idx = np.arange(4).reshape(-1, 1).astype(float)                                    # the "data" is just node numbers
def kernel(a, b):                                                                  # returns the weight between nodes
    return W[np.ix_(a.ravel().astype(int), b.ravel().astype(int))]

for alpha in (0.5, 0.9):
    m = LabelSpreading(kernel=kernel, alpha=alpha, max_iter=5000, tol=1e-9).fit(idx, [0, -1, -1, 1])
    print(alpha, m.label_distributions_[:, 0].round(3), m.transduction_)
# 0.5 [0.966 0.832 0.22  0.036] [0 0 1 1]    -> the shares of class A we found by hand (96.6%, 83.2%, about 22%, 3.6%)
# 0.9 [0.728 0.652 0.48  0.346] [0 0 1 1]

Now the real comparison on the handwritten-digits images, with only 50 labelled images (5 per digit) out of 1797, where I also flipped a share of those 50 labels to a wrong digit. Accuracy on the unlabelled images, averaged over 8 random choices of the labelled images (scikit-learn 1.9.0, kNN graph with 7 neighbours; other versions and seeds will differ a little):

from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.semi_supervised import LabelPropagation, LabelSpreading

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True); X = X / 16.0
# y_partial: 5 labelled images per digit, everything else -1 (some labels flipped to wrong digits in the noisy runs)

lp = LabelPropagation(kernel="knn", n_neighbors=7, max_iter=3000).fit(X, y_partial)
ls = LabelSpreading(kernel="knn", n_neighbors=7, alpha=0.95, max_iter=3000).fit(X, y_partial)   # raise max_iter: the default is only 30!

Reading the table honestly: (1) even with perfectly clean labels, Label Spreading with a large α was a little better (93.8% versus 91.8%). (2) With wrong labels, accuracy falls for both methods, and α around 0.95 gave the best result, about 2 to 5 points above Label Propagation. (3) The default α = 0.2 was no better than Label Propagation on noisy labels. (4) The soft clamp rarely fixed the wrong labelled points themselves: of the flipped labels, 0% were corrected at α ≤ 0.8 and only 2% to 5% at α = 0.95. Its gain came mostly from limiting how far the mistakes spread. So do not expect miracles: clean labels remain the best remedy.

5. Choosing between the two

SituationChoose
Your labels are perfectly reliable (a curated gold standard)Label Propagation (or Label Spreading with a small α); the labels are the truth and should be kept.
Labels may be noisy (crowd-sourcing, automatic labels, human disagreement)Label Spreading with a fairly large α (try 0.8 to 0.95), checking the result.
You want an extra tuning knob and can afford to validate itLabel Spreading: treat α like any hyper-parameter, and choose it by validating on a few extra labelled examples that you kept aside.
Very few labels and clearly separated classesEither works; the graph (γ or k) matters far more than the choice between them.

Both share the same limits: they need the cluster or manifold assumption to hold; the graph settings and feature scaling are decisive; a dense RBF graph is expensive on big data. And if you have enough labels, ordinary supervised learning will usually do as well or better, so semi-supervised methods pay off when labels are scarce.

🏷️ Crowd-labelled data

When labels come from many part-time annotators, some are wrong; a soft clamp softens the damage.

🤖 Automatic pseudo-labels

Labels produced by another model or by rules are imperfect by nature; treating them as suggestions rather than facts fits.

🩻 Medical images

A few expert-labelled scans plus a large unlabelled archive; experts disagree at times, so labels are not gospel.

📄 Text categorisation

Categorise a large corpus from a small set of tagged documents; user tags can be inconsistent.

🧬 Biological networks

Predict protein or gene function through interaction networks, where annotations are incomplete and sometimes contradictory.

🕸️ Graph data

Any problem that already has a graph (social, citation, road, molecule) can use spreading directly, without a kernel.

Key takeaways

  • Label Spreading = Label Propagation with a soft clamp: F ← α S F + (1 − α) Y0, where S = D−½ W D−½. Each point mixes its neighbours' opinion (weight α) with its original label (weight 1 − α).
  • Labelled points can change: in our chain, at α = 0.5 the labelled ends are 96.6% and 3.6% class A instead of exactly 100% and 0%; the unlabelled points end up at 83.2% and about 22%.
  • α is the trust dial: small keeps the given labels, large follows the neighbours. In the noisy five-node chain, the mistaken label was overruled only for α above about 0.9.
  • On digits with 50 labels, Label Spreading (α ≈ 0.95) beat Label Propagation by about 2 points with clean labels and 2 to 5 points with 10 to 30% wrong labels, but it rarely corrected the wrong labelled points themselves. The default α = 0.2 gave no advantage.
  • Raise max_iter (the default is 30), tune the graph and α, scale the features, and validate on a few extra labels.

1. New P = α × (S(P,A) × A's value + S(P,Q) × Q's value) + (1 − α) × P's original = 0.5 × (0.8165 × 1 + 0.4082 × 0) + 0.5 × 0 = 0.5 × 0.8165 = 0.408.

As α → 1: the original labels are re-injected with weight (1 − α) → 0, so they seed the iteration but the neighbours take over: the labelled points can be overruled by their surroundings, all values flatten towards the same mixture, and it takes many iterations to converge.
